\subsection{Comparison with External Methods}
\label{sec:external_comparison}

Two independent methods---OPTICS (min\_samples=5, $\xi$=0.05) and KDE (90th percentile threshold)---were evaluated on the same synthetic benchmark.
\phantomsection\label{tab:external_methods}
KDE achieved perfect precision (1.000) but extremely low recall (0.049, HS = 0.557); OPTICS yielded F1 = 0.150 (HS = 0.369).
MutClust-Hybrid maintained the highest hotspot-score (0.778); parameter sensitivity across 30 OPTICS and 36 KDE configurations confirmed this superiority is structural rather than parameter-dependent.

\textbf{Stage 1 summary.} MutClust-Hybrid achieves the best hotspot-score (0.778) on synthetic data, but all methods show reduced performance on real GISAID data (F1: 0.785 $\to$ 0.123) due to sparse H-score distributions, while maintaining biological validity (enrichment $>$ 6.9-fold).

\subsection{Statistical Validation}
\label{sec:statistical_validation}

Statistical robustness was assessed through bootstrap CI estimation, permutation tests, pairwise comparisons, and Fisher's exact test.
All bootstrap CIs use the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) method~\cite{efron1987better} ($n = 7$ functional regions).

\textbf{Permutation tests.}
Both random label shuffling (1,000 iterations, all methods $p < 0.001$) and circular permutation tests (200 iterations, preserving spatial autocorrelation) confirmed that all detection results are statistically significant ($z > 9.2$, $p < 0.005$ for all; e.g., MutClust-Hybrid: F1 = 0.785, null mean = 0.023, HS = 0.778, null = 0.251).

\textbf{Fisher's exact test.}
After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction ($q < 0.05$) on 128 comparisons, 3 were significant:
TC-freq-v2 functional enrichment (1.16-fold, $p_{\text{adj}}$ = 0.002),
TC-freq-v3 functional enrichment (1.59-fold, $p_{\text{adj}}$ = 0.023),
and CH-score convergent evolution enrichment (1.70-fold, $p_{\text{adj}}$ = 0.023).
Two additional comparisons reached $q < 0.10$.

\label{sec:sensitivity_analysis}

\textbf{Sample size.}
MutClust-Hybrid was evaluated across 100--10,000 sequences (5 replicates per size).
Hotspot-score stabilized at $\approx$0.62 by $n = 1{,}000$ (Spearman $\rho > 0.997$, SD $< 0.015$), with even $n = 100$ yielding HS $> 0.52$ ($\rho > 0.94$), indicating that $\sim$1,000 sequences suffice for stable results (Figure~\ref{fig:sample_size_convergence}).

\subsection{Temporal and Structural Robustness}
\label{sec:temporal}

\textbf{Temporal generalization (GISAID 2024--2025).}
\phantomsection\label{tab:temporal}
Analysis of 9,957 sequences from 2024--2025 revealed \textbf{H-score saturation}: all 1,273 Spike positions had nonzero H-scores (mean = 8.47, SD = 0.098), compared to only 114 in 2020--2021.
This eliminates discriminative power---all methods converge to base rate precision ($\sim$0.33), with MutClust-Fixed/dNdS detecting 3,819 positions in a single cluster and MutClust-Hybrid detecting 3,225 in 377 clusters.
The saturation reflects Omicron-era divergence from Wuhan-Hu-1, validating the limitation that H-scores against a fixed reference lose discriminative power over time.

\textbf{Indel exclusion impact.}
\label{sec:indel_impact}
H-score cannot capture insertions or deletions: 99.8\% of 2024--2025 sequences carry functional indels (del69-70, Y144del), yet all have H-score = 0.
The F1 impact is negligible ($\Delta$F1 = $-$0.002), but this structural limitation makes biologically important variants systematically invisible (discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\subsection{Phylogenetic Non-Independence Simulation}
\label{sec:phylo_simulation}

Coalescent-based simulations and real-data validation were performed to evaluate phylogenetic bias in H-scores.

\subsubsection{Simulation and Real-Data Validation}
\label{subsec:phylo_correction_poc}

In a coalescent simulation (100 replicates, $N = 500$), H-score ranked a single-origin founder mutation above convergent mutations in 100\% of replicates, whereas Fitch parsimony correctly ranked convergent mutations at the top ($\rho = -0.876$, $p = 1.28 \times 10^{-64}$).
\phantomsection\label{subsec:treetime_ml_validation}TreeTime~\cite{sagulenko2018treetime} ML validation ($N = 2{,}000$, 50 replicates) confirmed both Fitch and TreeTime-ML achieved AUC = 1.000 for convergent mutation discrimination, while H-score achieved AUC = 0.000.
\phantomsection\label{subsec:treetime_gisaid_validation}Real GISAID validation (500 sequences, 2024--2025) confirmed a weak negative correlation between H-score and homoplasy ($\rho = -0.107$, $p = 1.36 \times 10^{-4}$): P681 ranks 2nd by H-score but 1,134th by homoplasy (founder effect), while L452 and K417 have H-score = 0 but homoplasy of 91 (convergent evolution).
These results demonstrate that \textbf{frequency-based scores systematically conflate founder effects with positive selection}; D614G is accordingly excluded from Layers~A and~B~\cite{korber2020tracking}.

\subsubsection{Homoplasy-Based Scoring Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:homoplasy_sensitivity}

Seven detectors were evaluated on TreeTime ML homoplasy counts (500 sequences, 2020-H1) against Layer~A (25 positions; Table~\ref{tab:phylo_sensitivity}).
All except ScoreDBSCAN yield negative MCC (mean $-0.018$) due to temporal mismatch (pre-VoC sequences); D614G correctly received homoplasy = 0.
The Spearman correlation between H-score vs.\ homoplasy method rankings ($\rho = 0.036$, $p = 0.939$) confirms rankings are conditional on scoring system and temporal scope.

\section{Cross-Pathogen Large-Scale Benchmark}
\label{sec:9pathogen_results}

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}
\vspace{0.5em}

Building on the Stage~1 findings, this section reports the Stage~2 cross-pathogen benchmark results.
The benchmark evaluates \textbf{20 scoring types} (organized into 6 categories; see Table~\ref{tab:scoring_types} in Chapter~\ref{ch:mutbench}) combined with \textbf{39 detection algorithm variants} (from 14 families) across \textbf{11 pathogens}, totaling \textbf{8,580 evaluations}.
The 20 scoring types are derived from \textbf{10 underlying biological features}; some features generate multiple scoring variants (e.g., frequency yields \textit{freq}, \textit{freq\_with\_indel}, and \textit{rare\_freq}), while composite scorings combine multiple features (e.g., EVEscape composite, EqualWeight).
The subsequent information-type analysis (Section~\ref{sec:information_analysis}) examines the 10 underlying features to explain why scoring performance differs across pathogens.
For the feature analysis, \textbf{12 pathogens} were used (adding Zika, Rabies, and EV-A71 to increase statistical power in per-feature comparisons), though the main benchmark uses 11 pathogens.\footnote{Throughout this chapter, ``11 pathogens'' refers to the Stage~2 benchmark set (Zika excluded due to insufficient sequences, $n = 52$). The information-type analysis in Section~\ref{sec:information_analysis} uses 12 pathogens (including Zika) for increased statistical power in per-feature comparisons.}

Stage~2 uses MCC rather than hotspot-score because the stability component (Jaccard-based subsampling reproducibility) requires bootstrap validation that has not been cross-validated across all 11 pathogens with their widely varying alignment lengths (261--1,330 AA) and MSA sizes (530--5,019 unique sequences). MCC provides a single, well-established metric that is robust to class imbalance and does not require subsampling.

This section sequentially reports the benchmark scale, per-pathogen best method analysis, analysis of variance, cross-validation, and statistical test results.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Benchmark Scale and Data}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_scale}

This section presents the full-scale cross-pathogen benchmark results that constitute the primary evidence for the dissertation's second contribution (statistical demonstration of pathogen-adaptive method selection necessity).

A total of 8{,}580 evaluations (20 scoring formulas $\times$ 39 detection algorithms $\times$ 11 pathogens) were conducted.
The 20 scoring formulas span six information categories: frequency-based (freq, rare\_freq, freq\_with\_indel), MSA-derived (entropy, entropy\_with\_indel, homoplasy), phylogenetic (dN/dS proxy, FUBAR posterior probability, FUBAR positive selection, FUBAR Bayes factor), structural (pLDDT inverse, SASA, Grantham distance), AI-based (ESM-2 LLR, Tranception, semantic change, stability, stability\_structural), and composite (EVEscape composite, EqualWeight).
Each evaluation computed MCC, F1, precision, and recall against the 3-layer ground truth (Layer A: convergent evolution positions, Layer B: constrained regions, Layer C: DMS).
DMS Layer~C evaluation was conducted for 4 pathogens (H3N2, HIV-1, RSV, Dengue) where experimental fitness data are available.
All FASTA sequences were collected from NCBI GenBank using actual accession numbers.

While the 11/11 unique best combinations is notable, a permutation analysis shows that with $\binom{20 \times 39}{1} = 780$ possible scoring--detection family combinations, the probability of all 11 being unique by chance is $\prod_{i=0}^{10}(780-i)/780 \approx 0.929$ (approximately 93\%).
The more substantive finding is not the uniqueness itself but the large performance gap between oracle and generalized selection (mean MCC gap $= 0.265$ from LOPO cross-validation), which demonstrates that the \emph{correct} choice of combination matters significantly even though many combinations exist.

The key observations from these results are as follows.
First, the best scoring type differs fundamentally across pathogens, and this diversity spanning six information categories is the central finding. The best scoring types are biologically interpretable:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H3N2 --- FUBAR Bayes factor}: Influenza A HA is under strong diversifying selection at antigenic sites, and FUBAR detects pervasive positive selection across well-sampled phylogenies. H3N2's decades-long evolutionary history provides the deep phylogenetic signal that dN/dS-based methods require.
\item \textbf{Norovirus --- homoplasy}: Norovirus undergoes epochal evolution with periodic replacement of dominant genotypes, generating convergent mutations at antigenic sites across independent lineages---exactly the signal homoplasy measures.
\item \textbf{SARS-CoV-2 --- Tranception}: With a shallow pandemic phylogeny (originating $\sim$2019), frequency-based signals are dominated by founder effects. Protein language models capture broader evolutionary constraints learned from diverse coronavirus families, compensating for limited within-species phylogenetic depth.
\item \textbf{RSV --- ESM-2 LLR}: RSV F protein is under antibody-mediated selection, but the limited phylogenetic diversity of sequenced RSV strains weakens frequency and phylogenetic signals. ESM-2's learned evolutionary constraints provide an alternative information source.
\item \textbf{Rabies --- stability}: Rabies G protein mediates cell entry through a pH-triggered conformational change, making structural stability a key functional constraint. Positions where stability-altering mutations are tolerated indicate adaptive flexibility.
\item \textbf{EV-A71 --- semantic change}: Enterovirus VP1 evolves under immune pressure at the canyon rim. Semantic change (cosine distance in ESM-2 embedding space) captures how much a mutation shifts the protein's learned representation, detecting positions where substitutions have large functional consequences.
\item \textbf{HCV, HIV-1, Dengue, MERS --- entropy; Influenza B --- frequency}: For pathogens with extensive within-host diversity (HCV, HIV-1) or serotype-level variation (Dengue), Shannon entropy---which captures the richness of the residue distribution---outperforms raw frequency. Influenza B, with its two-lineage structure providing clear frequency differentiation, favors simple mutation frequency. MERS, with limited phylogenetic resolution from sparse sampling, also benefits from entropy's sensitivity to low-frequency variants.
\end{itemize}

Second, the best detection family also varies: KDE (4 pathogens), Wavelet (3), SlidingTest (1), Bayes (1), MutClust (1), and SWAN (1).
Third, the MCC range spans from 0.196 (RSV/MERS) to 0.660 (HCV), exhibiting large variation that reflects structural differences in mutation patterns across pathogens.
SARS-CoV-2 exhibits the lowest best MCC (0.211) despite being the best-studied pathogen, reflecting the extreme class imbalance (25 Layer A positions out of 1,259 total, a 2.0\% positive rate) combined with the shallow pandemic phylogeny that limits phylogenetic signal.

\textbf{Key finding}: The scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$) is the \textbf{largest source of variance, exceeding all main effects and other interactions}. Under Cohen's effect size benchmarks~\cite{cohen1988statistical}, $\omega^2 > 0.14$ constitutes a large effect. Critically, the scoring main effect ($\omega^2 = 0.063$) is 4.8$\times$ larger than the detection family main effect ($\omega^2 = 0.013$), demonstrating that \emph{what biological information is used} matters far more than \emph{which algorithm processes it}.

This finding directly resolves the third problem from Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}: the interaction dominance ($\omega^2 = 0.296$) provides definitive statistical proof that detection performance is fundamentally pathogen-dependent. Moreover, the shift from family$\times$pathogen dominance (Stage~2 preliminary with 9 scoring types: $\omega^2 = 0.285$) to scoring$\times$pathogen dominance (full 20-scoring benchmark: $\omega^2 = 0.296$) reveals that once a sufficiently diverse set of information sources is evaluated, the choice of biological information becomes the primary determinant.

\textbf{LOPO Cross-Validation.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_lopo}
LOPO (Leave-One-Pathogen-Out) cross-validation was performed to evaluate whether the best combination selected from 10 training pathogens generalizes to the excluded test pathogen (Figure~\ref{fig:lopo_crossval}).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Combination match rate}: 0/11 (0\%). In all 11 iterations, the training-best combination and the test oracle combination were mismatched.
\item \textbf{Mean generalization MCC}: 0.032. The test MCC of the training-best combination was near zero on average.
\item \textbf{Mean performance gap}: 0.265. The average MCC difference between the oracle and the generalized prediction was 0.265.
\end{itemize}

\label{fig:lopo_crossval}
\end{figure}

These results demonstrate that the 0/11 match rate is a \textbf{key finding}: the premise of a single best-performing method does not hold, providing the strongest statistical evidence for the necessity of pathogen-adaptive method selection.

\textbf{Friedman Test.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_friedman}
Using the 11 pathogens as blocks, a Friedman test was performed on the performance differences among the top 20 scoring--detection family combinations.

\begin{equation}
\chi^2 = 7.69, \quad p = 0.990
\end{equation}

Kendall's coefficient of concordance $W = \chi^2 / [n(k-1)] = 7.69 / (11 \times 19) = 0.037$, indicating near-zero agreement in method rankings across pathogens.
With $p = 0.990$ and $W = 0.037$, the result is \textbf{not} statistically significant, meaning that even the top 20 combinations cannot be statistically distinguished from each other across 11 pathogens. This is a stronger result than the previous 9-pathogen analysis: no combination is reliably better than any other, because each pathogen's performance landscape is fundamentally different. Combined with the LOPO 0/11 match rate, this provides definitive evidence that no universal best method exists.

\textbf{Pathogen-Level BCa Bootstrap Confidence Intervals.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_bootstrap}
The best single combination across all pathogens was evaluated using BCa bootstrap.
The selection criterion was the combination with the highest mean MCC among those whose 95\% BCa confidence interval lower bound exceeds 0.
Ranking by mean MCC alone could place combinations that perform well only on specific pathogens at the top (e.g., E\_only + SWAN achieves MCC = 0.311 on Norovirus but MCC = 0.000 on the remaining 8 pathogens); thus, the condition that the confidence interval excludes 0 ensures consistent performance across all pathogens.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Best single combination}: P*E + Wavelet ($t$ = 1.5)
\item \textbf{Mean MCC}: 0.124
\item \textbf{95\% BCa confidence interval}: [0.047, 0.202]
\end{itemize}

The mean MCC of the best single combination (0.124) is only 42\% of the mean oracle MCC (0.298), confirming that a single fixed combination falls substantially short. Despite appearing low, this MCC exceeds the random baseline (mean=0.001, SD=0.003) by approximately 41 standard deviations, and MCC $>$ 0.1 under extreme class imbalance (1--16\% positive rate) is not trivial.

\textbf{Subsampling Robustness.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_subsampling}
The benchmark was repeated at MSA sequence proportions of 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, and 100\%, and the overall pathogen-average MCC was stable in the range of 0.054--0.058.
The MCC variation with decreasing sample size was within $\pm$0.002, confirming that the benchmark results are robust to sequence sample size.
This supports the conclusion that the benchmark findings reflect intrinsic properties of pathogen mutation structures rather than artifacts of a particular sample size.

\subsection{Supplementary Analyses}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_supplementary}

Several supplementary analyses were conducted to validate the robustness and generalizability of the benchmark findings.

\textbf{Cross-Paradigm Validation: Protein Language Model Scoring.}
\label{subsec:esm_validation}
To test whether the benchmark findings generalize beyond MSA-derived scoring formulas, an independent scoring paradigm was evaluated: the ESM-2 protein language model (650M parameters)~\cite{lin2023esm2}. ESM-2 computes per-position log-likelihood ratios (ESM-LLR) from a single consensus sequence using learned evolutionary representations, requiring no multiple sequence alignment. This provides orthogonal validation of the benchmark framework because the scoring signal originates from a fundamentally different methodology---a deep neural network trained on millions of protein families---rather than from the same MSA used by the nine existing scoring formulas.

The 650M-parameter ESM-2 model was selected as it represents the largest single-GPU model in the ESM-2 family, balancing computational feasibility with representation capacity. Larger models (ESM-2 3B) require multi-GPU inference, while smaller models (8M, 35M) may lack sufficient capacity for proteins exceeding 1,000 residues.
ESM-LLR was computed for all 11 pathogens using wild-type marginal scoring: a single forward pass through the model yields $\text{ESM-LLR}[i] = -\log P(a_i \mid \text{context})$, where $a_i$ is the consensus amino acid at position $i$. For proteins exceeding the 1,022-token context window (SARS-CoV-2 Spike: 1,259 AA after MSA trimming; MERS Spike: 1,330 AA), a sliding window with 256-residue overlap was applied and scores were averaged in overlap regions. The resulting ESM-LLR arrays were evaluated through the same 39 detection methods used in the main benchmark.

\label{subsec:anova_diagnostics}

\subsection{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{subsec:pahd_motivation_summary}

Synthesizing the key results of the cross-pathogen benchmark, the need for pathogen-adaptive method selection is demonstrated by four independent lines of evidence.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Interaction dominance}: The scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$) is the largest source of variance, exceeding all main effects and other interactions. Which information source is used matters more than which algorithm processes it.
\item \textbf{Unique best combinations}: 11 unique best combinations were observed across 11/11 pathogens, with 9 distinct scoring types. No two pathogens share the same best combination.
\item \textbf{Generalization failure}: LOPO cross-validation yielded 0/11 combination matches with a mean generalization MCC of 0.032. The best combination selected from training pathogens does not generalize to new pathogens.
\item \textbf{No universally superior combination}: Friedman $\chi^2 = 7.69$, $p = 0.990$. No single combination is reliably distinguishable as superior across all pathogens.
\end{enumerate}

These results statistically establish that ``the best-performing method differs for each pathogen,'' and that the interaction between scoring method and pathogen is the dominant source of performance variance.
The following section investigates \textit{why} this interaction arises by analyzing the discriminative power of individual information types.

\section{Information-Type Analysis}
\label{sec:information_analysis}
\label{ch:adapt} % retained for cross-reference compatibility (formerly Chapter 5)

The 20 scoring formulas used in the benchmark are derived from 10 underlying biological features spanning 6 information categories. Several scoring formulas represent the same feature (e.g., freq, freq\_with\_indel, and rare\_freq are all frequency-based), while composite formulas (EVEscape\_composite, EqualWeight) combine multiple features. The following analysis examines the discriminative power of the 10 underlying features to explain why the scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction dominates.

The preceding sections demonstrated that the best-performing scoring--detection combination differs across pathogens ($\omega^2_{\text{scoring}\times\text{pathogen}} = 0.296$). This section investigates \textit{why} this interaction arises by analyzing the discriminative power of individual information types (features) that underlie the scoring formulas, and examines whether pathogen-specific feature importance patterns explain the observed cross-pathogen heterogeneity.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Per-Feature Discriminative Power}
\label{subsec:per_feature_auc}

Ten positional features were evaluated as individual predictors of Layer~A (convergent evolution) ground truth across all 11 pathogens. These features represent six distinct information categories:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Frequency-based} (3 features): \textit{freq} (proportion of sequences carrying a non-reference amino acid at each position), \textit{entropy} (Shannon entropy measuring allelic diversity), and \textit{rare\_freq} (frequency of minor alleles below 5\% threshold, capturing low-frequency variants that may signal emerging adaptive mutations).
\item \textbf{Phylogeny-based} (1 feature): \textit{homoplasy} (number of independent mutation origins inferred by Fitch parsimony on the phylogenetic tree, distinguishing convergent evolution from founder-effect-driven frequency).
\item \textbf{Evolutionary rate} (1 feature): \textit{dN/dS proxy} (nonsynonymous substitution rate normalized by codon degeneracy of the consensus amino acid; since only protein MSAs were available, this approximates true dN/dS without nucleotide codon alignment).
\item \textbf{Deep learning-based} (2 features): \textit{esm2\_llr} (ESM-2 protein language model log-likelihood ratio~\cite{lin2023esm2,rives2021biological}, measuring how surprising a mutation is relative to learned evolutionary constraints from 250M protein sequences) and \textit{semantic change} (mean cosine distance between wild-type and mutant ESM-2 embeddings at each position~\cite{hie2021learning}, measuring how much a mutation alters the protein's functional context).
\item \textbf{Structure-based} (2 features): \textit{plddt} (AlphaFold2/ESMFold predicted local distance difference test score~\cite{jumper2021alphafold}, indicating structural confidence and disorder propensity) and \textit{SASA} (relative solvent accessible surface area computed from ESMFold-predicted structures using the Shrake--Rupley algorithm~\cite{shrake1973environment}, measuring surface exposure).
\item \textbf{Chemical property} (1 feature): \textit{Grantham distance} (mean physicochemical distance between the consensus and observed variant amino acids at each position~\cite{grantham1974amino}, capturing whether substitutions are conservative or radical).
\end{itemize}

These 10 features were selected from a broader set of 22 candidate information types identified from the literature. Features excluded from the analysis include: weighted contact number (WCN, high correlation with SASA, $r > 0.7$), intrinsic disorder prediction (largely redundant with pLDDT $< 50$~\cite{wilson2022alphafold}), co-mutation/mutual information (pairwise rather than per-position feature, incompatible with the univariate per-position analysis framework; additionally $O(L^2)$ computational cost), codon usage bias (weak association with hotspot status), and epitope/glycosylation distance (not uniformly available across all 11 pathogens). The selection criteria were: (1)~independence from existing features, (2)~applicability to all 11 pathogens, and (3)~prior validation in the literature.

\subsection{Feature Importance by Pathogen}
\label{subsec:rf_importance}

To assess feature importance in a multivariate context---where features may interact and provide complementary information---a Random Forest classifier~\cite{breiman2001random} (200 trees, max depth 5, balanced class weights, random state 42) was trained for each pathogen using the 10 features as predictors and Layer~A as the binary label. Feature importances were extracted as mean decrease in impurity (Gini importance), and model performance was evaluated via stratified 5-fold cross-validation AUC.

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_importance_grid} presents the feature importance distributions across all 11 pathogens. Three patterns are notable.

\textbf{First, the dominant feature differs across pathogens.}
For SARS-CoV-2, ESM-2 LLR dominates (importance = 0.331), followed by pLDDT (0.286) and the newly added SASA (0.202)---all three are non-frequency features. For H3N2, pLDDT remains the most important feature (0.338), with semantic change (0.105) providing complementary information from the deep learning category. For Norovirus, homoplasy dominates (0.345), consistent with its near-perfect univariate AUC (0.944). For HIV-1, ESM-2 LLR (0.214) and homoplasy (0.143) share importance. For HCV, the dN/dS proxy (0.145), homoplasy (0.139), and Grantham distance (0.116) contribute complementary information in the multivariate context, despite the high univariate correlation between dN/dS proxy and frequency ($\rho = 0.87$).

\textbf{Second, expanding from 6 to 10 features improves cross-validation AUC.}
Norovirus achieves the highest cross-validation AUC (0.899). SARS-CoV-2 improves from 0.554 (6 features) to 0.606 (10 features), and MERS improves from 0.537 to 0.875, demonstrating that the additional features---particularly SASA and semantic change---capture information relevant to hotspot prediction that was previously missing. RSV improves to 0.737, HCV to 0.701, and the remaining pathogens achieve: H3N2 (0.791), HIV-1 (0.802), Dengue (0.782), Influenza~B (0.756).

\textbf{Third, the importance rankings differ from univariate AUC rankings.}
For HIV-1, entropy has the highest univariate AUC (0.730) but ranks third in RF importance (0.127) behind ESM-2 LLR (0.214) and homoplasy (0.143), because entropy and frequency are highly correlated ($\rho = 0.97$, see Figure~\ref{fig:feature_correlation}) and share importance in the RF model while ESM-2 LLR provides non-redundant information. This demonstrates that multivariate analysis reveals complementary feature contributions that univariate analysis cannot capture.

\subsection{Feature Correlation Structure}
\label{subsec:feature_correlation}

Understanding the correlation structure among the 10 features is essential for interpreting both the univariate and multivariate results. Figure~\ref{fig:feature_correlation} presents the mean pairwise Spearman correlation across all 11 pathogens.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{feature_correlation_heatmap.png}
\caption{Mean pairwise Spearman correlation among 10 features across 11 pathogens. Three clusters are evident: frequency-derived features (freq, entropy, rare variant, dN/dS proxy) are highly correlated ($\rho > 0.54$); deep learning features (ESM-2 LLR, semantic change) show weak negative correlation ($\rho = -0.09$); and structure-based features (pLDDT, SASA) are weakly anti-correlated ($\rho = -0.19$). Homoplasy is the most independent feature ($|\rho| < 0.23$ with all others).}
\label{fig:feature_correlation}
\end{figure}

Three key patterns emerge. First, frequency, entropy, and dN/dS proxy form a highly correlated cluster ($\rho_{\text{freq--entropy}} = 0.97$, $\rho_{\text{freq--dN/dS}} = 0.87$), confirming that the protein-level dN/dS proxy shares substantial information with frequency-based features due to the absence of nucleotide-level synonymous substitution data (a limitation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Second, Grantham distance and semantic change show moderate correlation ($\rho = 0.52$), reflecting the relationship between physicochemical dissimilarity and embedding-space distance. Third, homoplasy is the most independent feature ($|\rho| < 0.23$ with all other features), explaining its unique discriminative power for pathogens where convergent evolution is a strong signal.

The correlation structure explains why expanding from 6 to 10 features yields differential improvements: SASA ($\rho = -0.19$ with pLDDT) provides genuinely independent structural information, while dN/dS proxy ($\rho = 0.87$ with frequency) adds limited independent signal. The largest improvements from the expanded set occur for pathogens where the new independent features (SASA, semantic change) are informative---notably MERS (CV AUC: 0.537 $\rightarrow$ 0.875) and SARS-CoV-2 (0.554 $\rightarrow$ 0.606).

\subsection{Implications for Adaptive Method Selection}
\label{subsec:info_type_implications}

The per-feature, Random Forest, and correlation analyses collectively provide a mechanistic explanation for the scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$) observed in the ANOVA (Section~\ref{subsec:9pathogen_statistical}). Different pathogens require different \textit{information types} for effective hotspot discrimination, and scoring formulas that emphasize the relevant information type for a given pathogen will produce higher-quality input signals for downstream detection algorithms.

The cross-pathogen benchmark demonstrated that the best scoring--detection combination differs across pathogens (11/11 unique best combinations, 9 distinct scoring types). The feature importance analysis reveals that this heterogeneity is rooted in pathogen-specific relationships between biological features and hotspot status: H3N2 hotspots are best detected by FUBAR phylogenetic selection scores, Norovirus hotspots by homoplasy (convergent evolution), SARS-CoV-2 hotspots by protein language model scores (Tranception), RSV by ESM-2 LLR, EV-A71 by semantic change, Rabies by stability predictions, and HCV/Influenza~B/MERS by frequency-based scores.

The Random Forest analysis demonstrates that combining multiple features in a multivariate model achieves cross-validation AUC values up to 0.899 (Norovirus) and 0.875 (MERS), suggesting that the performance ceiling can be raised by integrating complementary information types into the scoring stage rather than relying on a single formula.

The following subsections extend this analysis by quantifying individual feature contributions through ablation, examining ground truth heterogeneity as a potential confound, and evaluating practical search space reduction through multi-source integration.

\subsection{Practical Validation: Search Space Reduction}
\label{subsec:search_space_reduction}

The analysis results above demonstrate that multiple information types contribute to hotspot prediction, but the most informative feature differs by pathogen. This subsection evaluates whether integrating all 10 features reduces the experimental search space for hotspot validation.

The integration method uses equal-weight combination: each feature is z-score normalized across all $L$ alignment positions, directional signs are applied (to handle inverse associations such as pLDDT AUC $< 0.5$ for MERS), and the weighted combination $s_i = \sum_{f=1}^{10} (1/10) \cdot \mathrm{sign}(\hat{s}_f) \cdot z_{i,f}$ is computed. Positions exceeding a percentile threshold are called as hotspots. Equal weighting was chosen because, with only 12 reference pathogens, adaptive weighting strategies introduce more noise than signal (see below).

Leave-One-Pathogen-Out (LOPO) cross-validation was conducted across all 12 pathogens, comparing EqualWeight against frequency-only (FreqThresh) and random baselines.
Under the EqualWeight top-10\% threshold, mean MCC = 0.083 (9/12 pathogens positive), outperforming FreqThresh (0.075, 8/12 positive) and random (0.000, 5/12 positive).

\textbf{Vaccine escape position validation.}
To assess practical relevance, integrated-detection hotspots were compared against experimentally confirmed vaccine/antibody escape positions for H3N2 (29 antigenic drift sites in HA sites~A and~B) and SARS-CoV-2 (15 RBD + NTD vaccine escape positions).

This transforms hotspot detection from an exhaustive search into a guided investigation, providing a ranked candidate list that directs limited experimental resources toward the most promising positions.

\subsection{Adaptive Weighting: Current Limits and Future Potential}
\label{subsec:adaptive_weighting_limits}

Three adaptive weighting strategies were evaluated against the equal-weight baseline: kNN-weighted ($k{=}3$ inverse-distance interpolation of RF importance weights using a 13-dimensional pathogen profile), Top-3 AUC (equal weight on 3 highest-AUC features from nearest neighbors), and Correlation-aware ensemble (equal weight on 5 lowest-correlation features with AUC $> 0.5$).
All adaptive strategies performed worse than EqualWeight: kNN-weighted achieved a maximum mean MCC of 0.020, Top-3 AUC 0.049, and Correlation-aware 0.043, compared to EqualWeight's 0.083.

This underperformance is explained by the sparsity of the reference database.
With only 12 reference pathogens (11 available in LOPO), the 13-dimensional pathogen profile space is severely undersampled (dimensions-to-samples ratio $\approx 1.1$), making $k$NN matching unreliable and introducing noise rather than adaptation.

However, the oracle strategy (mean MCC = 0.160) approximately doubles EqualWeight performance (0.083), confirming that per-pathogen weighting provides genuine improvement when labeled data is available.
Closing this gap without labels requires expanding the reference database to 20--30+ pathogens for meaningful $k$NN matching in the pathogen profile space.
This is a finding about the current state of available data, not a failure of the adaptive concept; the oracle ceiling demonstrates that the potential for adaptive improvement exists and motivates knowledge base expansion as a concrete future direction.

\subsection{Information-Type Analysis Summary}
\label{subsec:info_analysis_summary}

The multi-source information integration analysis yields four key findings:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Feature heterogeneity}: The most discriminative information type differs across pathogens (5 distinct best features among 12 pathogens), providing a mechanistic explanation for the scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$) observed in the ANOVA.
\item \textbf{Compact core}: A 4-feature core (homoplasy, pLDDT, entropy, frequency) captures 98\% of the full 10-feature ensemble performance under uniform weighting, while features that are noise on average (ESM-2, SASA) are the best discriminators for specific pathogens.
\item \textbf{Practical search space reduction}: Equal-weight 10-feature integration achieves statistically significant H3N2 vaccine escape enrichment (4.012$\times$, $p = 0.0023$) where single-feature approaches fail to reach significance, transforming exhaustive experimental screening into guided investigation.
\item \textbf{Adaptive weighting potential}: The oracle ceiling (MCC = 0.160 vs.\ EqualWeight 0.083) confirms that per-pathogen feature weighting can double performance, but requires expanding the reference database beyond the current 12 pathogens.
\end{itemize}

The broader implications for adaptive method selection, including knowledge base scaling requirements and future extensions, are discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.
