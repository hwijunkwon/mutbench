\subsection{Comparison with External Methods}
\label{sec:external_comparison}

Two independent methods---OPTICS (min\_samples=5, $\xi$=0.05) and KDE (90th percentile threshold)---were evaluated on the same synthetic benchmark.
\phantomsection\label{tab:external_methods}
KDE achieved perfect precision (1.000) but extremely low recall (0.049, HS = 0.557); OPTICS yielded F1 = 0.150 (HS = 0.369).
MutClust-Hybrid maintained the highest hotspot-score (0.778); parameter sensitivity across 30 OPTICS and 36 KDE configurations confirmed this superiority is structural rather than parameter-dependent.

\textbf{Stage 1 summary.} MutClust-Hybrid achieves the best hotspot-score (0.778) on synthetic data, but all methods show reduced performance on real GISAID data (F1: 0.785 $\to$ 0.123) due to sparse H-score distributions, while maintaining biological validity (enrichment $>$ 6.9-fold).

\subsection{H3N2 time-stratified prospective sanity check}
\label{subsec:h3n2_temporal_pilot}

To audit the prospective signal of the simplest scoring baseline---a question raised in connection with benchmark deployability---a one-pathogen time-stratified pilot was performed on H3N2~HA. The 9{,}000 H3N2 sequences in this benchmark partition naturally into three 5-year periods (2010--2015, 2016--2020, 2021--2025; $n=3{,}000$ each). Frequency scores were computed on the 2010--2015 training window only, then evaluated against (a) the static Layer~A antigenic-site reference (Koel~2013; 25 sites; retrospective) and (b) positions newly emerging in each later window, where ``newly emerging'' is defined as freq~$<5\%$ in 2010--2015 and freq~$\geq 10\%$ in the holdout window (prospective).

Table~\ref{tab:h3n2_temporal_pilot} summarizes the pilot. Frequency-based ranking captures the static Layer~A antigenic sites at $2.7\times$--$3.4\times$ enrichment (Fisher one-sided $p$ from $0.017$ to $0.053$ across top-20/30/50)---consistent with the Stage~2 H3N2 cross-sectional result. However, prospective enrichment against newly emerged variants is essentially zero: $0/20$, $0/30$, and $0/50$ for the six positions emerging in 2016--2020, and only $1/50$ for the seven positions emerging in 2021--2025 (Fisher $p \to 1$). The 2016--2020 emergent set includes H3N2 position~137 (a Layer~A antigenic site that re-emerged at higher frequency) and five novel positions (147, 187, 342, 422, 500); the 2021--2025 emergent set lies entirely outside Layer~A. Because this is a single-pathogen one-cycle pilot using only frequency, the interpretation is conservative: \textbf{frequency alone, trained on a 2010--2015 window, does not predict positions that emerge in subsequent 5-year windows beyond chance}, even though it does identify the historical Layer~A antigenic sites retrospectively. This negative result is consistent with the framing in Chapters~\ref{ch:results}--\ref{ch:discussion}: it motivates the multi-source approach (homoplasy + pLDDT + entropy + frequency 4-feature core; Section~\ref{subsec:feature_ablation}) precisely because frequency alone is retrospectively diagnostic but prospectively weak. Full multi-source prospective re-evaluation across all 11 pathogens is identified as Priority~1 future work (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).

\subsection{Multi-pathogen sliding-window prospective backtest}
\label{subsec:sliding_window_backtest}

The H3N2 frequency-only pilot (Section~\ref{subsec:h3n2_temporal_pilot}) is generalised to a multi-pathogen sliding-window prospective backtest. For each of the 11 pathogens, per-sequence year metadata is extracted from the codon-aligned NCBI~CDS FASTA descriptions (year-tagged sequence yield: H3N2/Influenza~B/SARS-CoV-2 100\%, EV-A71 98\%, MERS/Norovirus 84\%, HCV 81\%, Dengue 64\%, HIV-1 63\%, Rabies 53\%, RSV 51\%; Zika nucleotide CDS unavailable, so Zika is replaced by its codon alignment of partial-sequence proteins which lack year annotation and is therefore omitted from the backtest). For each split year $T_0$ that satisfies $n_{\text{train}}\geq 30$ and $n_{\text{test}}\geq 20$, training and test windows are formed by year~$<T_0$ and year~$\geq T_0$ respectively. Per-position frequency and entropy are recomputed on the training window, and a uniform-weight $z$-ensemble of (freq, entropy) is the prospective scorer---these are the two time-varying features in the dissertation's 4-feature core (the static features homoplasy and pLDDT live in a different MSA coordinate frame and are therefore not re-trained per window). The reference set for each window is the same ``newly emerging'' definition as the H3N2 pilot: positions with consensus stable in train (freq~$<5\%$) but a non-consensus allele rising to freq~$\geq 10\%$ in test. Top-30 MCC, F1, AUROC, and one-sided Fisher enrichment are computed per window.

Across the 219 evaluated windows spanning 11 pathogens, the per-pathogen mean AUROC of the freq+entropy ensemble is 0.79 for Rabies, 0.77 for EV-A71, 0.70 for Influenza~B, 0.60 for MERS, 0.56 for RSV, 0.51 for SARS-CoV-2, and below 0.50 for the remaining four (Dengue 0.48, HIV-1 0.46, H3N2 0.43, Norovirus 0.32, HCV 0.31). Two pathogens (EV-A71, Rabies) show meaningful prospective signal in mean Top-30 MCC (0.146 and 0.112 respectively) and Top-30 enrichment ($10.8\times$ for EV-A71, $4.1\times$ for Rabies); the other nine show near-zero or slightly negative MCC (between $-0.10$ and $0.00$), consistent with the H3N2 pilot's near-chance prospective enrichment. Linear-trend slope of MCC vs.\ split year (concept-drift estimate) is small in magnitude across all pathogens (range $-0.006$ to $+0.032$ MCC/year), so the limited prospective signal is not deteriorating systematically over time but is bounded throughout the observed period. The grand mean across pathogens is MCC~$=-0.006$, AUROC~$=0.539$, mean Top-30 enrichment~$1.57\times$ for the freq+entropy ensemble, with freq-only and entropy-only producing essentially identical aggregates ($\Delta$AUROC~$<0.01$). Table~\ref{tab:sliding_window_backtest} summarises per-pathogen results, and Figure~\ref{fig:sliding_window_backtest} shows the per-pathogen MCC trajectory across split years.

The interpretation parallels the H3N2 pilot: \textbf{the time-varying portion of the 4-feature core (freq+entropy) detects newly emerging high-frequency sites at clearly above-chance rates only for two of eleven pathogens (EV-A71 AUROC 0.77, Rabies 0.79), with one further pathogen partially above chance (Influenza~B AUROC 0.70)}. For the remaining eight pathogens, training on early-year sequences and testing on later-year sequences yields essentially chance-level prospective discrimination at the position level. This is consistent with the dissertation's Section~\ref{sec:plm_data_limitations} finding that frequency-feature discriminative power is itself non-stationary across 6-month windows on SARS-CoV-2 Spike, and with the pathogen-heterogeneity result of Section~\ref{subsec:9pathogen_statistical} (interaction $\eta^2 = 0.296$). The static features in the full 4-feature core (homoplasy, pLDDT) cannot be re-trained per window in the present coordinate frame and are therefore not exercised by this backtest; the operational implication is that the dissertation's retrospective MCC headline ($0.341$ exact, $0.454$ at $\pm 10$~window; Table~\ref{tab:region_overlap_mcc}) does not directly transfer to the forward-time setting at the position level for most pathogens, and the documented forward-time signal is concentrated in EV-A71, Rabies, and to a lesser extent Influenza~B. Section~\ref{sec:prospective_validation_gap} updates the limitations discussion accordingly.

\paragraph{4-feature core extension (3-/4-feature ensembles).}
\label{para:sliding_window_4feature}
The freq+entropy ensemble above re-trains only the two time-varying members of the 4-feature core. To probe whether the static members (pLDDT, homoplasy) carry residual prospective signal, the canonical pre-computed pLDDT and homoplasy scores from \texttt{feature\_matrix\_*.csv} are projected onto the codon-aligned MSA frame via Needleman--Wunsch alignment of the per-pathogen consensus sequences (mean mapping coverage $0.83$, range $0.24$--$0.98$; full diagnostics in \texttt{sliding\_window\_prospective\_4feature\_diagnostics.csv}). pLDDT enters as $-\text{pLDDT}$ so that high score corresponds to low confidence (emergence-prone), matching the dissertation's feature-importance signs (Section~\ref{subsec:rf_importance}); homoplasy is treated as a window-static prior because re-running TreeTime ML per window is intractable for the 11-pathogen panel (this is a documented limitation, not a covert leakage: the homoplasy score is computed once over the full MSA and is therefore identical across all training windows of a given pathogen). Across the same 73 windows with at least one emergent position, the grand-mean Top-30 MCC for freq+entropy is $-0.0009$, freq+entropy+pLDDT is $-0.0060$, freq+entropy+homoplasy is $+0.0042$, and the full 4-feature ensemble (freq+entropy+pLDDT+homoplasy) is $-0.0097$; the corresponding grand-mean AUROCs are $0.555$, $0.543$, $0.549$, and $0.561$. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (per-window MCC) versus the freq+entropy baseline give $p=0.170$ (+pLDDT, mean $\Delta=-0.005$), $p<0.001$ (+homoplasy, mean $\Delta=+0.005$, 44 better/$5$ worse/$24$ tied across 73 windows), and $p=0.016$ (4-feature, mean $\Delta=-0.009$); thus +homoplasy yields a small but statistically distinguishable improvement at the per-window level, while pLDDT alone and the 4-feature combination slightly degrade the Top-30 MCC despite a marginally higher mean AUROC. Per-pathogen, the most pronounced gains are for EV-A71 (mean MCC $0.146 \to 0.188$ with homoplasy; AUROC $0.773 \to 0.844$), Rabies (AUROC $0.789 \to 0.860$ with homoplasy), and RSV (AUROC $0.560 \to 0.922$ with the 4-feature ensemble, $n=2$ windows); HCV's pLDDT addition raises AUROC from $0.31$ to $0.58$, but adding homoplasy collapses it to $0.09$, indicating that homoplasy is destructive in the HCV regime where the empirical homoplasy distribution is essentially flat. These results are consistent with the retrospective LOPO finding that no single static feature wins across all pathogens (Section~\ref{subsec:9pathogen_lopo}): adding static priors helps in EV-A71/Rabies/RSV, hurts in HCV, and is approximately neutral elsewhere. \subsection{Benchmark Scale and Data}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_scale}

This section presents the full-scale cross-pathogen benchmark results that constitute the primary evidence for the dissertation's second contribution: statistical demonstration that the most predictive biological information source is pathogen-dependent.

A total of 8{,}580 evaluations (20 scoring formulas $\times$ 39 detection parameter variants from 14 families $\times$ 11 pathogens) were conducted.
The 20 scoring formulas span six information categories: frequency-based (freq, rare\_freq, freq\_with\_indel), MSA-derived (entropy, entropy\_with\_indel, homoplasy), phylogenetic (dN/dS proxy, FUBAR posterior probability, FUBAR positive selection, FUBAR Bayes factor), structural (pLDDT inverse, SASA, Grantham distance), AI-based (ESM-2 masked-marginal score, Tranception, semantic change, stability, stability\_structural), and composite (EVEscape composite, EqualWeight).

DMS Layer~C evaluation was conducted for 6 pathogens (SARS-CoV-2, H3N2, HIV-1, RSV, Rabies, EV-A71) where experimental fitness data are available.
All FASTA sequences were collected from NCBI GenBank using actual accession numbers.

While the 11/11 unique best combinations is notable, a permutation analysis shows that with $\binom{20 \times 39}{1} = 780$ possible scoring--detector-variant combinations, the probability of all 11 being unique by chance is $\prod_{i=0}^{10}(780-i)/780 \approx 0.932$ (approximately 93\%).
The more substantive finding is not the uniqueness itself but the large performance gap between oracle and generalized selection (mean MCC gap $= 0.265$ from LOPO cross-validation), which demonstrates that the \emph{correct} choice of combination matters materially even though many combinations exist (the LOPO 0/11 match rate itself is null-consistent under permutation; ``materially'' refers to the magnitude of the oracle--generalized gap, not to a hypothesis test on LOPO).

While the finding that different pathogens favor different methods might appear self-evident, the non-trivial contribution lies in three aspects: (1)~quantifying the magnitude of this interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$, explaining 29.6\% of variance---far exceeding the scoring main effect of 6.3\%); (2)~demonstrating that the information \textit{type} (not the detection algorithm) is the dominant modeled factor; and (3)~quantifying the cost of ignoring pathogen identity through the oracle-vs-generalized MCC gap of 0.265 (LOPO 0/11 match rate is itself null-consistent under permutation and is reported as corroboration rather than independent evidence), motivating pathogen-aware information-source selection rather than a single universal method.

\paragraph{Worst-end disclosure (negative-MCC and degenerate combinations).}
\label{para:stage2_worst_mcc}
For symmetry with the per-pathogen \emph{best} reported in Table~\ref{tab:stage2_best}, the corresponding worst-end summary across the full 8{,}580-evaluation grid (\texttt{stage3\_full\_results.csv}) is also reported here so that readers can audit the floor of the distribution alongside the headline ceiling. The single worst (scoring, detector, pathogen) combination in the grid is HCV $\times$ \emph{stability} $\times$ SWAN ($w$=50, $k$=1.0) at MCC $= -0.361$ (precision $= 0.000$, recall $= 0.000$, 139 detected positions; \texttt{stage3\_full\_results.csv}, row index 6782). Aggregating across all 8{,}580 cells, 4{,}171 cells (48.6\%) attain MCC $< 0$, and 4{,}695 cells (54.7\%) attain MCC $\le 0$; the worst-mean detector families are ScoreDBSCAN ($\epsilon = 15$, mean MCC $-0.016$; $\epsilon = 8$, mean MCC $-0.013$) and the worst-mean scoring channels are \emph{plddt\_inv} (mean $-0.017$), \emph{grantham} ($-0.008$), and the Tranception channel ($-0.006$, primarily because Tranception requires PLM-suitable depth; on shallow MSAs it underperforms frequency by a wide margin). This worst-end count is consistent with the headline finding: a near-half negative-MCC fraction is exactly what pathogen-dependent optimality predicts---a single fixed scoring-detector combination is statistically expected to underperform the pathogen-specific MCC-optimal choice on the majority of pathogens, so most cells in the 8{,}580-grid are below their per-pathogen MCC-best by a wide margin. The honest reading is therefore that the pathogen-adaptive headline (Table~\ref{tab:stage2_best}, $\omega^2 = 0.296$) is supported \emph{not} by an absence of bad cells but by the large gap between the per-pathogen ceiling (Table~\ref{tab:stage2_best}, MCC range 0.196--0.660) and the cell-level floor (MCC down to $-0.361$); the $\sim$48.6\% negative-MCC fraction is the operational cost of choosing a wrong (scoring, detector) combination for a given pathogen and is exactly the cost that pathogen-aware selection avoids.

The key observations from these results are as follows.
First, the best scoring type differs fundamentally across pathogens, and this diversity spanning four of six information categories (frequency-based, MSA-derived, phylogenetic, and AI-based) is the central finding. The best scoring types are biologically interpretable:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H3N2 --- FUBAR Bayes factor}: Influenza A HA is under strong diversifying selection at antigenic sites, and FUBAR detects pervasive positive selection across well-sampled phylogenies. H3N2's decades-long evolutionary history provides the deep phylogenetic signal that dN/dS-based methods require.
\item \textbf{Norovirus (homoplasy, AUC 0.944)}: Norovirus undergoes epochal evolution with periodic replacement of dominant genotypes (GII.4 variants), generating convergent mutations at antigenic sites across independent lineages. The near-perfect AUC reflects the fact that homoplasy directly measures this convergent signal, making it an almost ideal predictor for this evolutionary pattern. This contrasts sharply with frequency-based features (AUC 0.760), which conflate founder-effect frequency with genuine convergent evolution.
\item \textbf{SARS-CoV-2 --- Tranception}: With a shallow pandemic phylogeny (originating $\sim$2019), frequency-based signals are dominated by founder effects. Protein language models capture broader evolutionary constraints learned from diverse coronavirus families, compensating for limited within-species phylogenetic depth---a finding independently corroborated by the SARS-CoV-2--specific CoVFit PLM~\cite{covfit2025}, which fine-tunes ESM-2 on surveillance and DMS data and reports analogous PLM-driven gains over frequency-only baselines.
\item \textbf{RSV --- ESM-2 masked-marginal score}: RSV F protein is under antibody-mediated selection, but the limited phylogenetic diversity of sequenced RSV strains weakens frequency and phylogenetic signals. ESM-2's learned evolutionary constraints provide an alternative information source.
\item \textbf{Rabies --- stability}: Rabies G protein mediates cell entry through a pH-triggered conformational change, making structural stability a key functional constraint. Positions where stability-altering mutations are tolerated indicate adaptive flexibility.
\item \textbf{EV-A71 --- semantic change}: Enterovirus VP1 evolves under immune pressure at the canyon rim. Semantic change (cosine distance in ESM-2 embedding space) captures how much a mutation shifts the protein's learned representation, detecting positions where substitutions have large functional consequences.
\item \textbf{HCV --- frequency}: HCV's extreme within-host diversity generates a quasispecies cloud with high variability at most positions, paradoxically making simple mutation frequency the most discriminative feature: true hotspot positions accumulate mutations at measurably higher rates than the already-elevated background, and this signal is captured more cleanly by frequency than by entropy, which saturates at highly variable positions.
\item \textbf{HIV-1 --- entropy}: HIV-1 maintains large intra-host quasispecies populations where functionally important positions under immune pressure exhibit diverse residue distributions rather than a single dominant variant. Shannon entropy captures this diversity signal, outperforming raw frequency which is confounded by the high baseline mutation rate across the HIV-1 genome.
\item \textbf{Dengue --- entropy\_with\_\allowbreak indel}: Dengue circulates as four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1--4), each maintaining independent evolutionary trajectories. The serotype-level variation generates high positional diversity at envelope protein epitopes that mediate serotype-specific and cross-reactive antibody responses. The \texttt{entropy\_with\_indel} variant outperforms standard entropy because indel events at inter-domain linker regions of the E protein contribute to structural rearrangements relevant to receptor binding and immune escape, and excluding these events loses biologically meaningful signal.
\item \textbf{MERS --- frequency}: MERS-CoV has limited phylogenetic resolution due to sparse and episodic sampling (fewer than 500 publicly available spike sequences, primarily from zoonotic spillover events rather than sustained human-to-human transmission). With such sparse data, more sophisticated scoring types (entropy, phylogenetic methods) lack the statistical power to produce reliable estimates, while simple mutation frequency---despite its general limitations---provides the most stable signal. The few positions with elevated frequency correspond to receptor-binding positions on the S1 subunit that mediate DPP4 binding and camel-to-human adaptation, where recurrent zoonotic spillover has generated detectable frequency peaks.
\item \textbf{Influenza B --- frequency}: Influenza B circulates as two antigenically distinct lineages (Victoria and Yamagata) that have co-circulated since the 1980s. Unlike H3N2, which undergoes continuous antigenic drift requiring frequent vaccine updates, Influenza B's two-lineage structure creates a bimodal frequency distribution where lineage-defining mutations at key HA antigenic sites exhibit clear frequency differentiation. This stable lineage architecture makes simple mutation frequency an effective discriminator, whereas phylogenetic methods like FUBAR---which excel for H3N2's continuous drift---provide less additional information when the dominant evolutionary signal is lineage divergence rather than ongoing positive selection.
\end{itemize}

Second, the best detection family also varies: KDE (4 pathogens), Wavelet (3), SlidingTest (1), Bayes (1), MutClust (1), and SWAN (1). The FUBAR spotlight analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:fubar_spotlight}) illustrates how phylogenetic scoring can outperform all other information types for specific pathogens such as H3N2.
Third, the MCC range spans from 0.196 (RSV/MERS) to 0.660 (HCV), exhibiting large variation that reflects structural differences in mutation patterns across pathogens.
SARS-CoV-2 (0.211), RSV (0.196), and MERS (0.196) occupy the lowest tier of best MCC values. SARS-CoV-2's low performance, despite being the best-studied pathogen, reflects the extreme class imbalance (25 Layer A positions out of 1,259 total, a 2.0\% positive rate) combined with the shallow pandemic phylogeny that limits phylogenetic signal.
Additionally, the Layer~A ground truth for SARS-CoV-2 includes only positions with documented convergent evolution across multiple SARS-CoV-2 lineages, excluding positions that are functional but lineage-specific. This strict criterion reduces the number of true positives available for detection, further lowering the achievable MCC.

\textbf{Key finding}: the scoring~$\times$~pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$ at the evaluation level, $N = 8{,}580$; 95\% cluster-bootstrap CI [0.195, 0.333]) is the largest modeled component, exceeding all main effects and other two-way interactions and qualifying as large under Cohen's $\omega^2 > 0.14$ threshold~\cite{cohen1988statistical}. The within-cell residual ($\omega^2 \approx 0.39$, absorbing the three-way interaction and parameter-variant noise; SS partition derived from the same Type~II ANOVA fit reported in Table~\ref{tab:stage2_anova}, with the residual quantified as $1 - \sum \omega^2_{\text{modeled}} - \omega^2_{\text{cluster~SE}}$) is comparable in magnitude, so ``largest source'' in this dissertation reads as ``largest modeled component.'' The companion \emph{cell-level} estimate (re-fit on the 3{,}080 unique scoring~$\times$~family~$\times$~pathogen cells; Section~\ref{sec:scoring_input_heterogeneity} of Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}) is $\omega^2 = 0.234$, with an ANCOVA-adjusted value of 0.264 after controlling for $\log(\text{protein length})$ and Layer-A positive rate, and a Bayesian partial-pooling posterior of 0.252--0.319 (95\% HDI [0.188, 0.396]). The cell-level 0.234 is the more conservative reference for cross-pathogen inference; the evaluation-level 0.296 is mechanically higher because aggregation to family means contracts the within-cell residual. The qualitative conclusion (scoring~$\times$~pathogen as the dominant modeled source) holds in all four parameterizations.

The three dominant components---scoring~$\times$~pathogen (0.296), pathogen (0.117), and family~$\times$~pathogen (0.082)---together account for $\sim$50\% of total variance.

The same 8{,}580-evaluation dataset is re-examined under three statistical lenses: ANOVA (interaction $\omega^2 = 0.296$, 11-pathogen cluster-bootstrap 95\% interval [0.195, 0.333]); Friedman ($\chi^2 = 7.69$, $p = 0.990$); LOPO (0/11 matches, gap 0.265). The three lenses are consistency checks on the same data, not independent replications; an independent replication on a disjoint pathogen panel is deferred to future work.

This finding directly addresses the third problem from Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}: the interaction dominance ($\omega^2 = 0.296$ at 20-type granularity; 0.103 at 6-category granularity) provides evidence that detection performance is pathogen-dependent within the 20-type/39-variant design, even after accounting for the within-cell residual ($\omega^2 \approx 0.39$). Moreover, the shift from family$\times$pathogen dominance (Stage~2 preliminary with 9 scoring types: $\omega^2 = 0.285$) to scoring$\times$pathogen dominance (full 20-scoring benchmark: $\omega^2 = 0.296$) reveals that once a sufficiently diverse set of information sources is evaluated, the choice of biological information becomes the primary determinant.

\textbf{Friedman Test.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_friedman}
Using the 11 pathogens as blocks, a Friedman test was performed on the performance differences among the top 20 scoring--detection family combinations.

\begin{equation}
\chi^2 = 7.69, \quad p = 0.990
\end{equation}

Kendall's coefficient of concordance $W = \chi^2 / [n(k-1)] = 7.69 / (11 \times 19) = 0.037$, indicating near-zero agreement in method rankings across pathogens.
With $p = 0.990$ and $W = 0.037$, the result is \textbf{not} statistically significant, meaning that even the top 20 combinations cannot be statistically distinguished from each other across 11 pathogens.
This non-significance admits two interpretations: (1)~all methods perform similarly poorly (universal poor performance), or (2)~each pathogen favors a different method, so rankings are inconsistent across pathogens (pathogen-dependent optimality). The Friedman test's power at $n = 11$ blocks is limited (approximately 0.15 for medium effect $W$), so the non-significance is consistent with both readings; this should therefore be read as corroboration of, rather than disconfirmation of, pathogen-dependent optimality.
The LOPO performance gap of 0.265 MCC (family-level oracle mean 0.297 vs.\ generalized mean 0.032) and the per-pathogen oracle MCC range (0.196--0.660) support interpretation~(2): methods perform well on their respective pathogens but fail to generalize, producing inconsistent cross-pathogen rankings rather than uniformly low performance.

\textbf{Nemenyi pairwise post-hoc test.} Because the omnibus Friedman result cannot reject ($p = 0.990$), we report the Nemenyi pairwise post-hoc test on the same 20 combinations $\times$ 11 pathogens rank matrix to surface any residual differential ranking signal that the omnibus might mask. None of the $\binom{20}{2} = 190$ pairs reach the conventional threshold (raw $\alpha = 0.05$: $0/190$ pairs; Bonferroni-adjusted $\alpha = 2.6 \times 10^{-4}$: $0/190$); the entire pairwise $p$-value distribution lies in $[0.989, 1.000]$ with median $1.000$. The most differentiated pair is \texttt{freq+AdaptWin} (mean rank $8.59$) vs.\ \texttt{entropy+Wavelet} (mean rank $12.64$, rank gap $4.05$, $p = 0.989$); the most similar pair is \texttt{dnds\_proxy+AdaptWin} vs.\ \texttt{homoplasy+AdaptiveKnee} (rank gap $0.18$, $p = 1.000$). The post-hoc null-non-rejection is therefore consistent with the omnibus and confirms that the $n = 11$ rank-block design does not have power to discriminate any pair of top-20 combinations, even at unadjusted~$\alpha$. The pairwise structure is informative in one auxiliary respect: the lowest-mean-rank (i.e., best-performing) cluster is dominated by frequency- and selection-pressure-based combinations (\texttt{freq+AdaptWin}, \texttt{homoplasy+KDE}, \texttt{freq+SWAN}, \texttt{dnds\_proxy+SWAN}, \texttt{freq+SlidingTest}; mean ranks $8.59$--$9.73$), while the highest-mean-rank cluster is dominated by Bayesian-selection and Wavelet-detection combinations (\texttt{fubar\_pos\_sel+KDE}, \texttt{entropy+Wavelet}; mean ranks $12.55$--$12.64$). This descriptive ordering reproduces the qualitative ranking from the ANOVA scoring-main-effect ranking but the Nemenyi result formally certifies that, at $n = 11$, even the largest rank gap is statistically indistinguishable from chance. The full $20 \times 20$ pairwise $p$-value matrix and the long-form pair list are archived as \texttt{nemenyi\_pairwise\_pvalues.csv} and \texttt{nemenyi\_summary.csv} (with provenance JSON \texttt{nemenyi\_provenance.json}) in the \texttt{results/mutbench/} bundle.

\textbf{Permutation null for the 0/11 match rate and the 0.265 gap.}
At the detection-family level (280 scoring--family combinations; matching the 0.265 gap and generalized MCC = 0.032 reported above), two permutation analyses (10{,}000 resamples each) were performed to quantify what the observed LOPO statistics imply under a null of uninformative cross-pathogen training.
First, the 0/11 exact-match rate is itself expected under the null: with 280 combinations and 11 pathogens, $P(\text{matches}=0) \approx 0.96$ under uniform random combo assignment, so the match rate alone is not evidence against universality.
Second, the mean generalized MCC under random combo selection is $+0.017$ (SD $\approx 0.034$ for a single combo applied to all pathogens; SD $\approx 0.027$ for an independent random combo per pathogen). The observed training-best generalized MCC of $+0.032$ exceeds this null mean but only by approximately $0.4$--$0.6$ SD, yielding a one-sided $p \approx 0.25$--$0.28$. The 0.265 gap is therefore consistent with, but not statistically distinguishable from, a null in which training-based selection provides no cross-pathogen transfer.
This tempering does not invalidate the pathogen-adaptive conclusion: the $\omega^2 = 0.296$ interaction effect, the 9 distinct optimal scoring types across 11 pathogens, and the HIV-1 anchor of the vaccine-escape audit together speak to pathogen-specific optimality through complementary routes. But it clarifies that LOPO-0/11 and the 0.265 gap, read in isolation, are weaker evidence than the surface reading suggests, and that independent replication on additional pathogens is needed to sharpen the statistical support for cross-pathogen training's inability to transfer.

These auxiliary numbers are not reported as headline findings because each carries a small-sample caveat ($n = 10$, $n = 9$, $n = 4$), but they are recorded so that readers and reviewers can audit selective-reporting concerns directly.

\textbf{Pathogen-Level BCa Bootstrap Confidence Intervals.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_bootstrap}
The best single combination across all pathogens was evaluated using BCa bootstrap.
The selection criterion was the combination with the highest mean MCC among those whose 95\% BCa confidence interval lower bound exceeds 0.
Ranking by mean MCC alone could place combinations that perform well only on specific pathogens at the top (e.g., E\_only + SWAN achieves MCC = 0.311 on Norovirus but MCC = 0.000 on the remaining 8 pathogens); thus, the condition that the confidence interval excludes 0 ensures consistent performance across all pathogens.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Best single combination}: P*E + Wavelet ($t$ = 1.5)
\item \textbf{Mean MCC}: 0.124
\item \textbf{95\% combination-level BCa CI} (1{,}000 resamples over 11 pathogens): [0.047, 0.202]
\end{itemize}

The mean MCC of the best single combination (0.124) is only 36\% of the per-combination oracle MCC (0.341 at the scoring~$\times$~detector-variant grid), confirming that a single fixed combination falls substantially short. Despite appearing low, this MCC exceeds the random baseline (mean = 0.001, SD = 0.003; per-pathogen means computed from the 780-combination random-assignment distribution underlying the LOPO permutation null --- see permutation-null block above; the per-replicate panel-mean draws are queued for release as a supplementary CSV with the Zenodo provenance bundle) by approximately 41 standard deviations, and MCC $>$ 0.1 under extreme class imbalance (0.7\%--15.9\% positive rate) is not trivial.\footnote{The SD of 0.003 quoted here is the standard deviation \emph{of the panel-mean MCC} across 11 pathogens for a single random scoring--detection combination (i.e., the standard error of an 11-pathogen mean), which is distinct from the SD $\approx 0.034$ quoted earlier for the LOPO permutation null. The earlier SD = 0.034 is the spread of single-combination single-pathogen MCC values under random combo assignment with one combination applied to all 11 pathogens; the SD = 0.003 here is the spread of the resulting 11-pathogen \emph{means}. The two quantities differ by approximately a factor of $\sqrt{11} \approx 3.3$ for the panel-mean SE, plus an additional contraction from averaging across the 780 per-pathogen combinations sampled when forming the random-combination distribution.}

\textbf{Subsampling Robustness.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_subsampling}
The benchmark was repeated at MSA sequence proportions of 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, and 100\%, and the overall pathogen-average MCC was stable in the range of 0.054--0.058.
The MCC variation with decreasing sample size was within $\pm$0.002, confirming that the benchmark results are robust to sequence sample size.
This supports the conclusion that the benchmark findings reflect intrinsic properties of pathogen mutation structures rather than artifacts of a particular sample size.

\subsection{Supplementary Analyses}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_supplementary}

Several supplementary analyses were conducted to validate the robustness and generalizability of the benchmark findings.

\textbf{Cross-Paradigm Validation: Protein Language Model Scoring.}
\label{subsec:esm_validation}
To test whether the benchmark findings generalize beyond MSA-derived scoring formulas, an independent scoring paradigm was evaluated: the ESM-2 protein language model (650M parameters)~\cite{lin2023esm2}. In this benchmark, the ESM-2 channel uses a per-position masked-marginal pseudo-log-likelihood / mutation-surprise score from a single consensus sequence (reported under the conventional shorthand ESM-LLR), requiring no multiple sequence alignment. This provides an orthogonal scoring signal because it originates from a deep neural network trained on millions of protein families rather than from the same MSA statistics used by most other scoring formulas.

The 650M-parameter ESM-2 model was selected as it represents the largest single-GPU model in the ESM-2 family, balancing computational feasibility with representation capacity. Larger models (ESM-2 3B) require multi-GPU inference, while smaller models (8M, 35M) may lack sufficient capacity for proteins exceeding 1,000 residues.
ESM-LLR was computed for all 11 pathogens using wild-type marginal scoring: a single forward pass through the model yields $\text{ESM-LLR}[i] = -\log P(a_i \mid \text{context})$, where $a_i$ is the consensus amino acid at position $i$. For proteins exceeding the 1,022-token context window (SARS-CoV-2 Spike: 1,259 AA after MSA trimming; MERS Spike: 1,330 AA), a sliding window with 256-residue overlap was applied and scores were averaged in overlap regions. The resulting ESM-LLR arrays were evaluated through the same 39 detection methods used in the main benchmark.

In the full 20-scoring benchmark, the ESM-2 masked-marginal channel achieves the best MCC for RSV (0.196) and ranks among the top 3 for SARS-CoV-2, but performs poorly for Norovirus and HIV-1, reinforcing the central finding that no single scoring paradigm dominates across all pathogens. Spearman correlation between ESM-2 and MSA-based scores varies substantially across the per-position vectors of each pathogen (sample size $N$ = number of alignment positions, ranging from $N = 340$ for HCV to $N = 1{,}330$ for MERS): Norovirus ($N \approx 540$) shows strong positive correlation ($\rho = 0.63$, $p < 10^{-60}$), while HIV-1 ($N \approx 856$) shows moderate negative correlation ($\rho = -0.35$, $p < 10^{-14}$); per-pathogen $N$ matches the alignment lengths reported in Table~\ref{tab:per_feature_auc} caption. These $\rho$ values confirm that ESM-2 provides a distinct scoring signal for most pathogens.

\textbf{ANOVA Diagnostic Tests.}
\label{subsec:anova_diagnostics}
The Shapiro-Wilk test on ANOVA residuals rejected normality ($W = 0.974$, $p < 10^{-29}$), and Levene's test rejected homoscedasticity for all factors.
However, the non-normality is mild (residual skewness = 0.14, excess kurtosis = 2.83), and the large sample size ($N = 8{,}580$) ensures ANOVA robustness.
A limitation of this design is that the 39 parameter variants within each (scoring, detection family, pathogen) cell are not fully independent, sharing the same input MSA and ground truth; the effective number of independent units is closer to the number of unique cells ($20 \times 14 \times 11 = 3{,}080$) than $N = 8{,}580$.
The $\omega^2$ estimates should therefore be interpreted as indicative effect sizes rather than precise variance decompositions. The cluster bootstrap reported below is explicitly the effective-$N$-aware correction: resampling is performed \emph{at the pathogen level} (11 independent clusters) rather than at the evaluation level ($N = 8{,}580$), which naturally absorbs the within-pathogen dependence. Specifically, 1{,}000 cluster-bootstrap resamples over the 11 pathogens yield a 95\% CI of $[0.195, 0.333]$ for the scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction; this CI width (0.138) already reflects the effective-$N$ contraction and does not require an additional $\sqrt{N/N_{\text{eff}}}$ correction. An archived re-implementation (\texttt{scripts/cluster\_bootstrap\_omega.py}, \texttt{random\_seed=42}, $N_{\text{boot}} = 1{,}000$) reproduces the headline value 0.296 and yields a slightly wider 95\% percentile CI of $[0.201, 0.346]$ on the same 11-pathogen panel; this range overlaps the original interval, sits well above Cohen's large-effect threshold (0.14), and is provided as a verifiable provenance record (\texttt{results/mutbench/cluster\_bootstrap\_omega.csv}, \texttt{cluster\_bootstrap\_omega\_summary.csv}).
For sensitivity, a naive $N = 8{,}580$ bootstrap (ignoring pathogen-cluster structure) yields a narrower CI of $[0.260, 0.321]$; the wider cluster-bootstrap CI is the more honest reference for inference about the pathogen-dependence conclusion. The effect remains above Cohen's large-effect threshold ($\omega^2 > 0.14$) under both bootstrap schemes and at the more conservative 6-category aggregation. The 11-cluster regime is below the rule-of-thumb $\geq 30$ for percentile-bootstrap CI calibration, and a wild-cluster bootstrap~\cite{cameron2008bootstrap} is the standard sharper alternative for $G \leq 30$. \textbf{Wild-cluster bootstrap robustness.} We have re-estimated the headline interval under a wild-cluster (Rademacher-weight) bootstrap on the same 8{,}580-row grid: residuals from the restricted ANOVA that omits the scoring~$\times$~pathogen interaction are re-signed cluster-by-cluster (one Rademacher draw per pathogen), and the same Type-II-like $\omega^2$ decomposition is recomputed for $B = 1{,}000$ replicates (\texttt{scripts/wild\_cluster\_bootstrap\_omega.py}, \texttt{random\_seed = 42}; archived as \texttt{results/mutbench/wild\_cluster\_bootstrap\_omega.csv} and \texttt{wild\_cluster\_bootstrap\_omega\_summary.csv}). The wild-cluster 95\% percentile interval is $[0.201, 0.303]$ (mean $0.269$, median $0.276$, CI width $0.103$); both schemes give $\Pr(\omega^2_{\text{int}} \leq 0.14) = 0$ at the present replicate count. The wild-cluster lower bound matches the standard cluster bootstrap's archive-run lower bound to three decimal places ($0.201$ vs.\ $0.201$); the wild-cluster interval is narrower at the upper tail ($0.303$ vs.\ $0.346$) rather than at the lower tail, which is the expected sharper-but-not-uniformly-wider behaviour of the CGM construction when the standard percentile bootstrap's anti-conservatism manifests primarily as right-tail over-coverage near the headline. The pathogen-dependence finding therefore survives both small-cluster inferential frames; the lower bound ($\approx 0.20$) sits well above Cohen's large threshold ($\omega^2 > 0.14$) under either scheme. \textbf{Phylogenetic block bootstrap and mixed-effects triangulation.} As a further small-cluster honesty check we have additionally fit two model-based companions to the two cluster-bootstrap frames above. \emph{(i)~Phylogenetic block bootstrap (Felsenstein 1985 spirit):} the 11 pathogens are partitioned into 8 ICTV viral families (Coronaviridae \{SARS-CoV-2, MERS\}; Orthomyxoviridae \{H3N2, Influenza B\}; Flaviviridae \{HCV, Dengue\}; Picornaviridae, Caliciviridae, Retroviridae, Pneumoviridae, Rhabdoviridae each contributing a single pathogen), and the bootstrap unit is the family rather than the individual pathogen, so that phylogenetic non-independence within a family is preserved within each replicate. The block-bootstrap 95\% percentile interval is $[0.202, 0.346]$ (mean $0.272$, median $0.269$, $B = 1{,}000$, $\Pr(\omega^2 \leq 0) = 0$, $\Pr(\omega^2 \leq 0.14) = 0.008$); this lower bound matches the standard cluster bootstrap to three decimal places and the upper bound matches it as well, indicating that the headline conclusion is robust to the choice of phylogenetic versus pathogen exchangeability unit. \emph{(ii)~Linear mixed-effects model:} the same 8{,}580-row grid is fit as $\mathrm{MCC} \sim \mathrm{scoring}_{\text{fixed}} + (1\,|\,\mathrm{pathogen}) + (1\,|\,\mathrm{scoring{:}pathogen})$ in \texttt{statsmodels.MixedLM} (REML; \texttt{scripts/block\_bootstrap\_mixed\_effects\_omega.py}). The pathogen random-intercept variance is $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{pathogen}} = 1.28 \times 10^{-3}$, the scoring$\,\times\,$pathogen variance component is $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{int}} = 3.78 \times 10^{-3}$, and the residual variance is $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{resid}} = 6.07 \times 10^{-3}$, giving a model-based pseudo-$\omega^2_{\text{int}} = 0.340$ that overlaps the four bootstrap intervals. The likelihood-ratio test against the OLS null with no pathogen random effect gives $\chi^2_1 = 913.7$, $p < 10^{-198}$ (Self--Liang 1987 boundary correction), which independently confirms that pathogen-level variation is not attributable to noise. Combined, the four frequentist/model-based robustness frames (standard cluster, wild-cluster, phylogenetic block, mixed-effects) agree on a large pathogen-dependent interaction and are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:omega_robustness_5way} and the supplementary archive \texttt{results/mutbench/omega\_robustness\_5way.csv}. \emph{(iii)~Bayesian factor analysis (cycle~6 Tier~E1):} as a fifth, fully Bayesian inferential frame, a two-way random-effects factor analysis is fit on the same 8{,}580-row evaluation grid as $y_{ijk} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu + \alpha_i + \beta_j + \gamma_{ij},\, \sigma_{\text{resid}})$ with $\alpha_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{scoring}})$, $\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{pathogen}})$, $\gamma_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{int}})$, half-Normal hyperpriors (scale 0.5 on the factor sds, 0.2 on the residual sd) and a non-centered (Matt-trick) reparameterization (PyMC 5.28; \texttt{scripts/bayesian\_factor\_analysis\_omega.py}; 4~chains $\times$ 2{,}000 draws after 2{,}000 tuning steps; \texttt{random\_seed=42}; $\hat R = 1.003$ on $\omega^2_{\text{int}}$, ESS$_{\text{bulk}} = 618$, no post-tuning divergences). The variance-component posterior gives $\omega^2_{\text{int}} = \hat\sigma^2_{\text{int}} / (\hat\sigma^2_{\text{scoring}} + \hat\sigma^2_{\text{pathogen}} + \hat\sigma^2_{\text{int}} + \hat\sigma^2_{\text{resid}})$ with posterior mean $0.316$, 95\% HDI $[0.242, 0.388]$, and posterior probability $\Pr(\omega^2_{\text{int}} > 0.14) = 0.9998$. The HDI lower bound (0.242) sits above the three frequentist-bootstrap lower bounds ($\approx 0.201$--$0.202$) and the cycle-1 evaluation-level Bayesian model's HDI lower bound (0.188 / 0.246), so all five inferential frames concur that the lower bound is well above Cohen's large-effect threshold.

The mean top-1 precision is 0.338 and recall is 0.552, with substantial heterogeneity across pathogens (e.g., HCV achieves precision 0.964 but MERS shows precision 0.045 with perfect recall), confirming the limitations of single-threshold detection. The MERS row should be read as a \emph{failure case} rather than a success: perfect recall is achieved only because the top-1 detector flags 200 of the 1{,}330 alignment positions ($\approx 22\%$ of the protein), so all 9~Layer-A positives are recovered by near-indiscriminate calling. This is symptomatic of threshold and parameter saturation under the small Layer-A positive count ($n_{\text{A}} = 9$) for MERS rather than of a meaningful detection signal, and is not a basis for claiming successful generalization to MERS.

\label{subsec:dms_evolution_correlation}

In practice, when Layer~A and Layer~C indicate different optimal scoring types, the choice depends on the research objective: Layer~A rankings should be prioritized for immune evasion surveillance, while Layer~C rankings are more appropriate for functional impact assessment such as DMS-guided drug target prioritization.

\subsection{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{subsec:pahd_motivation_summary}

The cross-pathogen benchmark supports pathogen-adaptive method selection through two primary analyses---the scoring~$\times$~pathogen $\omega^2$ interaction, and a single-anchor vaccine-escape enrichment audit (HIV-1 as primary external anchor, with H3N2 self-consistency and SARS-CoV-2 exploratory checks)---plus two null-consistent corroborations (LOPO 0/11 and Friedman). The four lenses re-parameterize the same dataset and are not independent replications.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Interaction dominance (modeled)}: Among the modeled factors, the scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$; 95\% cluster-bootstrap CI [0.195, 0.333]) is the largest component, exceeding all main effects and other two-way interactions; a within-cell residual $\omega^2 \approx 0.39$ captures the unmodeled three-way interaction and parameter-variant noise. The optimal information source is pathogen-dependent.
\item \textbf{Unique best combinations}: 11 unique best combinations were observed across 11/11 pathogens, with 9 distinct scoring types. No two pathogens share the same best combination.
\item \textbf{LOPO null-consistent corroboration}: LOPO cross-validation yielded 0/11 combination matches with a mean generalization MCC of 0.032 and a 0.265 MCC oracle-vs-generalized gap (one-sided permutation $p \approx 0.25$--$0.28$ against the random-combination null). Both the match rate and the gap are null-consistent (failure to reject the null of universality); LOPO is therefore reported as \emph{corroboration} of the ANOVA interaction rather than as independent positive evidence.
\item \textbf{No universally superior combination}: Friedman $\chi^2 = 7.69$, $p = 0.990$ (a second failure-to-reject under the rank-block test, with limited power at $n = 11$ blocks). No single combination is reliably distinguishable as superior across all pathogens.
\end{enumerate}

The four lenses are not independent confirmations: only the ANOVA (within the modeled subspace) provides positive evidence of pathogen-dependence; the LOPO permutation test and the Friedman rank test are two failures-to-reject that are \emph{consistent} with pathogen-dependent optimality but do not by themselves constitute independent evidence. The HIV-1 vaccine-escape audit provides a separate external positive line. The next section investigates \textit{why} the ANOVA interaction arises by analysing individual information types.

\section{Information-Type Analysis}
\label{sec:information_analysis}
\label{ch:adapt} % retained for cross-reference compatibility (formerly Chapter 5)

The 20 scoring formulas used in the benchmark are derived from 10 underlying biological features spanning 6 information categories. Several scoring formulas represent the same feature (e.g., freq, freq\_with\_indel, and rare\_freq are all frequency-based), while composite formulas (EVEscape\_composite, EqualWeight) combine multiple features. The following analysis examines the discriminative power of the 10 underlying features to explain why the scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction dominates.

The preceding sections demonstrated that the best-performing scoring--detection combination differs across pathogens ($\omega^2_{\text{scoring}\times\text{pathogen}} = 0.296$). This section investigates \textit{why} this interaction arises by analyzing the discriminative power of individual information types (features) that underlie the scoring formulas, and examines whether pathogen-specific feature importance patterns explain the observed cross-pathogen heterogeneity.

\subsection{Per-Feature Discriminative Power}
\label{subsec:per_feature_auc}

Ten positional features were evaluated as individual predictors of Layer~A ground truth across all 11 benchmark pathogens. These features represent six distinct information categories:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Frequency-based} (3 features): \textit{freq} (proportion of sequences carrying a non-reference amino acid at each position), \textit{entropy} (Shannon entropy measuring allelic diversity), and \textit{rare\_freq} (frequency of minor alleles below 5\% threshold, capturing low-frequency variants that may signal emerging adaptive mutations).
\item \textbf{Phylogeny-based} (1 feature): \textit{homoplasy} (number of independent mutation origins inferred by Fitch parsimony on the phylogenetic tree, distinguishing convergent evolution from founder-effect-driven frequency).
\item \textbf{Evolutionary rate} (1 feature): \textit{dN/dS proxy} (nonsynonymous substitution rate normalized by codon degeneracy of the consensus amino acid; since only protein MSAs were available, this approximates true dN/dS without nucleotide codon alignment).
\item \textbf{Deep learning-based} (2 features): \textit{esm2\_llr} (ESM-2 masked-marginal mutation-surprise score~\cite{lin2023esm2,rives2021biological}, measuring how surprising a mutation is relative to learned evolutionary constraints from 250M protein sequences) and \textit{semantic change} (mean cosine distance between wild-type and mutant ESM-2 embeddings at each position~\cite{hie2021learning}, measuring how much a mutation alters the protein's functional context).
\item \textbf{Structure-based} (2 features): \textit{plddt} (AlphaFold2/ESMFold predicted local distance difference test score~\cite{jumper2021alphafold}, indicating structural confidence and disorder propensity) and \textit{SASA} (relative solvent accessible surface area computed from ESMFold-predicted structures using the Shrake--Rupley algorithm~\cite{shrake1973environment}, measuring surface exposure).
\item \textbf{Chemical property} (1 feature): \textit{Grantham distance} (mean physicochemical distance between the consensus and observed variant amino acids at each position~\cite{grantham1974amino}, capturing whether substitutions are conservative or radical).
\end{itemize}

These 10 features were selected from a broader set of 22 candidate information types identified from the literature. Features excluded from the analysis include: weighted contact number (WCN, high correlation with SASA, $r > 0.7$), intrinsic disorder prediction (largely redundant with pLDDT $< 50$~\cite{wilson2022alphafold}), co-mutation/mutual information (pairwise rather than per-position feature, incompatible with the univariate per-position analysis framework; additionally $O(L^2)$ computational cost), codon usage bias (weak association with hotspot status), and epitope/glycosylation distance (not uniformly available across all 11 pathogens). The selection criteria were: (1)~independence from existing features, (2)~applicability to all 11 pathogens, and (3)~prior validation in the literature.

\subsection{Feature Importance by Pathogen}
\label{subsec:rf_importance}

To assess feature importance in a multivariate context---where features may interact and provide complementary information---a Random Forest classifier~\cite{breiman2001random} (200 trees, max depth 5, balanced class weights, random state 42) was trained for each pathogen using the 10 features as predictors and Layer~A as the binary label. Feature importances were extracted as mean decrease in impurity (Gini importance), and model performance was evaluated via stratified 5-fold cross-validation AUC.\footnote{Caveat on CV design. The 5-fold split is at the position level within each pathogen, so adjacent residues with correlated features can land in different folds. This makes the reported AUC prone to spatial-autocorrelation leakage and should be interpreted as a feature-complementarity illustration rather than a generalisation estimate; a region-blocked or protein-domain-blocked CV is queued for future work.}

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_importance_grid} presents the feature importance distributions across all 11 pathogens. Three patterns are notable.

\textbf{First, the dominant feature differs across pathogens.}
For SARS-CoV-2, the ESM-2 masked-marginal channel dominates (importance = 0.331), followed by pLDDT (0.286) and the newly added SASA (0.202)---all three are non-frequency features. For H3N2, pLDDT remains the most important feature (0.338), with semantic change (0.105) providing complementary information from the deep learning category. For Norovirus, homoplasy dominates (0.345), consistent with its near-perfect univariate AUC (0.944). For HIV-1, ESM-2 (0.214) and homoplasy (0.143) share importance. For HCV, the dN/dS proxy (0.145), homoplasy (0.139), and Grantham distance (0.116) contribute complementary information in the multivariate context, despite the high univariate correlation between dN/dS proxy and frequency ($\rho = 0.87$).

\textbf{Second, expanding from 6 to 10 features improves cross-validation AUC.}
Norovirus achieves the highest cross-validation AUC (0.899). SARS-CoV-2 improves from 0.554 (6 features) to 0.606 (10 features), and MERS improves from 0.537 to 0.875, demonstrating that the additional features---particularly SASA and semantic change---capture information relevant to hotspot prediction that was previously missing. RSV improves to 0.737, HCV to 0.700, and the remaining pathogens achieve: H3N2 (0.791), HIV-1 (0.802), Dengue (0.782), Influenza~B (0.756).

\textbf{Third, the importance rankings differ from univariate AUC rankings.}
For HIV-1, entropy has the highest univariate AUC (0.730) but ranks third in Random Forest (RF) importance (0.127) behind ESM-2 (0.214) and homoplasy (0.143), because entropy and frequency are highly correlated ($\rho = 0.97$, see Figure~\ref{fig:feature_correlation}) and share importance in the RF model while ESM-2 provides non-redundant information. This demonstrates that multivariate analysis reveals complementary feature contributions that univariate analysis cannot capture.

\subsection{Implications for Pathogen-Aware Method Selection}
\label{subsec:info_type_implications}

The per-feature, Random Forest, and correlation analyses collectively provide a mechanistic explanation for the scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$) observed in the ANOVA (Section~\ref{subsec:9pathogen_statistical}). Different pathogens require different \textit{information types} for effective hotspot discrimination, and scoring formulas that emphasize the relevant information type for a given pathogen will produce higher-quality input signals for downstream detection algorithms.

The cross-pathogen benchmark demonstrated that the best scoring--detection combination differs across pathogens (11/11 unique best combinations, 9 distinct scoring types). These Stage~2 MCC-optimal scoring choices (Table~\ref{tab:stage2_best}) reflect pathogen-specific relationships between biological features and hotspot status: H3N2 hotspots are best detected by FUBAR phylogenetic selection scores, Norovirus hotspots by homoplasy (convergent evolution), SARS-CoV-2 hotspots by protein language model scores (Tranception), RSV by the ESM-2 masked-marginal channel, EV-A71 by semantic change, Rabies by stability predictions, and HCV/Influenza~B/MERS by frequency-based scores.
The complementary per-feature AUC analysis (Table~\ref{tab:per_feature_auc}) identifies, for each pathogen, which \textit{single biological feature} most discriminates Layer~A positions before any detection algorithm is applied; the two rankings need not agree because MCC further incorporates detection-algorithm interaction with the scoring input.

The Random Forest analysis demonstrates that combining multiple features in a multivariate model achieves cross-validation AUC values up to 0.899 (Norovirus) and 0.875 (MERS), suggesting that the performance ceiling can be raised by integrating complementary information types into the scoring stage rather than relying on a single formula.

The following subsections extend this analysis by quantifying individual feature contributions through ablation, examining ground truth heterogeneity as a potential confound, and evaluating practical search space reduction through multi-source integration. Importantly, the present work establishes the empirical basis for pathogen-aware selection; it does not claim a finished general-purpose adaptive algorithm for unseen pathogens.

\textbf{Cumulative addition} starting from the most discriminative feature (homoplasy) and adding features in AUC-rank order shows diminishing returns: 4 features (homoplasy, pLDDT, entropy, freq) achieve in-sample mean MCC = 0.081, similar to the in-sample full 10-feature mean of 0.083. A separate \emph{nested-LOPO} re-evaluation tests the same 4-feature core against the 10-feature ensemble under proper held-out-pathogen scoring (script: \texttt{scripts/feature\_ablation\_nested\_lopo.py}; CSV: \texttt{results/mutbench/feature\_analysis/feature\_ablation\_nested\_lopo.csv}; per-fold values in Table~\ref{tab:nested_lopo_4core}). The retention ratio computed on the LOPO scale is 0.081/0.070 = 1.16 (95\% CI [0.44, 1.48]), reflecting that the 6 features removed by the 4-core selection (rare\_freq, dN/dS, ESM-2 LLR, SASA, Grantham, semantic change) on average carry no positive contribution under uniform weighting, consistent with the leave-one-feature-out signs in Table~\ref{tab:feature_ablation}.

Two consequences for Contribution 3a follow. First, the operational claim that ``a compact 4-feature core matches the 10-feature ensemble under uniform weighting'' is supported under nested-LOPO with a permutation $p = 0.61$ on the paired delta and a 95\% CI for the delta that contains zero. Second, the original ``$\approx$98\% retention'' phrasing is not the cleanest summary of the nested-LOPO result: the LOPO ratio is itself centered above 1.0 with a wide CI, so the dissertation hereafter reports the result as ``operational equivalence under nested-LOPO ($p = 0.61$, paired delta CI [$-0.018$, $+0.042$])'' rather than as a percentage retention. The in-sample percentage (97.6\%) is retained only as a within-pathogen ablation descriptor and is no longer used as an out-of-pathogen generalization claim.

\subsection{Practical Validation: Search Space Reduction}
\label{subsec:search_space_reduction}

The analysis results above demonstrate that multiple information types contribute to hotspot prediction, but the most informative feature differs by pathogen. This subsection evaluates whether integrating all 10 features reduces the experimental search space for hotspot validation.

The integration method uses equal-weight combination, instantiated in two complementary protocols. The \emph{production} EqualWeight column underlying the headline scoring~$\times$~pathogen ANOVA (\texttt{scripts/run\_stage3\_full\_detectors.py}) applies pre-defined domain orientations (e.g., the inverse-pLDDT transform $\text{pLDDT}_{\max} - \text{pLDDT}$ rather than raw pLDDT) followed by uniform $1/10$ averaging over min--max normalised features, with no per-feature sign fit from labels at this stage. The \emph{nested-LOPO} ablation protocol used in Section~\ref{subsec:feature_ablation} (\texttt{scripts/feature\_ablation\_nested\_lopo.py}; Table~\ref{tab:nested_lopo_4core}) additionally fits a directional sign $\hat{s}_f \in \{-1, +1\}$ on the eleven training pathogens excluding the held-out pathogen and applies within-fold $z$-scoring; the held-out fold combination $s_i = \sum_{f=1}^{10} (1/10) \cdot \mathrm{sign}(\hat{s}_f) \cdot z_{i,f}$ shown above corresponds to this nested-LOPO protocol. The two protocols agree to within $\le 0.013$ MCC mean (production in-sample: 0.083; nested-LOPO: 0.070), so the directional sign step is not load-bearing for the headline cross-pathogen claim. Positions exceeding a percentile threshold are called as hotspots. Equal weighting was chosen because, with only 11 reference pathogens, adaptive weighting strategies introduce more noise than signal (see below).

Leave-One-Pathogen-Out (LOPO) cross-validation was conducted across the same 12-pathogen feature-ablation evaluation set used in Section~\ref{subsec:feature_ablation} (core 11 + Zika), comparing EqualWeight against frequency-only (FreqThresh) and random baselines.
Under the EqualWeight top-10\% threshold, mean MCC across the 12-pathogen set is 0.083, outperforming FreqThresh (0.075) and random ($\approx 0.000$); restricting to the core 11 pathogens shifts the baseline to 0.093 (consistent with the per-pathogen counts reported in Section~\ref{subsec:feature_ablation}, where 9 of 11 core pathogens carry positive EqualWeight MCC under the top-10\% threshold and 8 of 11 do for FreqThresh).

\textbf{Vaccine escape position validation.}
To assess practical relevance, the 20 scoring $\times$ 39 detector combinations were evaluated against experimentally confirmed vaccine/antibody escape positions for 3 pathogens: H3N2 (29 antigenic drift sites in HA sites~A and~B), SARS-CoV-2 (30 Spike escape sites in RBD, NTD, and S2), and HIV-1 (45 Env gp120 antibody escape sites). A total of 2,340 enrichment evaluations were performed (Table~\ref{tab:vaccine_escape_stage3}). Enrichment is defined as
\[
E = \frac{\text{observed overlap}}{\text{expected overlap}} = \frac{|D \cap A|}{|D|\,|A|/L},
\]
where $D$ is the set of detected hotspot positions, $A$ is the set of annotated escape positions, and $L$ is the protein length. Significance is tested by Fisher's exact test on the $2 \times 2$ table $\{|D \cap A|,\;|D \setminus A|,\;|A \setminus D|,\;L - |D \cup A|\}$. These three pathogens were selected on the single criterion of availability of curated antibody-escape annotations at the time of analysis; the remaining 8 pathogens are not included because comparable escape-position databases were not available.

\textbf{Key findings:}
All three pathogens achieve nominally significant vaccine escape enrichment ($p < 0.05$) with the optimal scoring--detection combination; after Bonferroni correction across 780 combinations, H3N2 and HIV-1 survive while SARS-CoV-2 is exploratory only. The best scoring \emph{category} for escape enrichment agrees with the Layer~A benchmark results at the category level (Phylogenetic for H3N2, Frequency for HIV-1), though the top scoring \emph{type} within that category can differ between the two evaluations (HIV-1 Layer~A best is \textit{entropy} with Wavelet(t{=}2.0), MCC 0.275; HIV-1 escape best is \textit{freq} with Wavelet(t{=}1.5), enrichment 7.19$\times$---both frequency-derived and correlated at $\rho \approx 0.97$, Section~\ref{sec:information_analysis}). The strength of this concordance as \emph{external} evidence is pathogen-dependent: HIV-1 provides the cleanest external validation (82\% of its escape set lies outside Layer~A; see circularity audit below), whereas for H3N2 the 69\% Layer~A/escape overlap means that the concordance is largely a self-consistency check rather than independent replication.

For H3N2, FUBAR Bayes factor captures 9 of 29 antigenic drift sites with 9.36-fold enrichment; 8 of those 9 captured positions are already in Layer~A (the audit paragraph below quantifies the resulting novel-only enrichment of 7.19$\times$ with Fisher $p = 0.132$, not significant). The multi-source EqualWeight integration achieves 4.012-fold enrichment ($p = 0.0023$), capturing positions in both antigenic site~A (position~137) and site~B (positions~155, 159, 160, 186, 188); within this top-5 integration comparison, frequency alone (FreqThresh top-5 $= \{137, 155, 159, 188\}$) recovers the most-frequent site~B positions but additionally misses positions 160 and 186 picked up by the EqualWeight integration. The miss therefore concerns specific within-site~B positions, not site~B as a whole.

The exclusion reasons fall into four categories: (i)~annotation granularity too coarse (Dengue, Influenza~B, Rabies, Norovirus); (ii)~sample size below the $n \geq 15$ threshold adopted for the three included pathogens (MERS, EV-A71); (iii)~confound with the benchmark's Layer~A set (RSV); and (iv)~mechanism mismatch (HCV: drug-resistance, not immune escape). No pathogen was excluded because of an expectation of low enrichment; the decision was made on data-availability grounds prior to running any enrichment computation. The generalizability of the enrichment finding to the other 8~pathogens therefore remains to be established, and the three validated pathogens should be read as a data-availability convenience sample rather than a balanced subset.

\textbf{Layer~A / escape-list overlap (circularity audit).}
Because Layer~A for H3N2, SARS-CoV-2, and HIV-1 is partly constructed from immune-escape-tagged positions (Chapter~\ref{ch:mutbench}, \texttt{layer\_a\_tags.csv} with an \texttt{immune\_escape} evidence tag), the vaccine-escape validation sets used in this section are not fully disjoint from the Layer~A ground truth. Concretely, the set overlap between the Layer~A immune-escape subset and the vaccine-escape validation list is 20/29 for H3N2 (69\% of the escape list is already in Layer~A), 18/30 for SARS-CoV-2 (60\%), and 8/45 for HIV-1 (18\%). A scoring method that excels on Layer~A therefore has a built-in advantage on H3N2 and SARS-CoV-2 escape enrichment, and the 9.36$\times$/7.63$\times$ values should be read as a self-consistency check rather than an independent replication on those two pathogens. HIV-1's 7.19$\times$ is the least affected by this circularity because 37 of 45 escape positions (82\%) are outside Layer~A and are genuinely novel relative to the benchmark ground truth. The qualitative conclusion---that benchmark-optimal scoring also best captures escape positions---holds, but the quantitative enrichment values for H3N2 and SARS-CoV-2 must be interpreted with this overlap in mind.

\textbf{Search space reduction.}
For a typical viral protein of 500 positions, the optimal scoring--detection combination narrows the candidate list to 20--80 positions with 7--9-fold enrichment for escape sites. This transforms hotspot detection from an exhaustive search into a guided investigation, providing a ranked candidate list that directs limited experimental resources toward the most promising positions.

\subsection{Adaptive Weighting: Current Limits and Future Potential}
\label{subsec:adaptive_weighting_limits}

Three adaptive weighting strategies were evaluated against the equal-weight baseline: kNN-weighted ($k{=}3$ inverse-distance interpolation of RF importance weights using a 13-dimensional pathogen profile), Top-3 AUC (equal weight on 3 highest-AUC features from nearest neighbors), and Correlation-aware ensemble (equal weight on 5 lowest-correlation features with AUC $> 0.5$).
All adaptive strategies performed worse than EqualWeight: kNN-weighted achieved a maximum mean MCC of 0.020, Top-3 AUC 0.049, and Correlation-aware 0.043, compared to EqualWeight's 0.083.

This underperformance is explained by the sparsity of the reference database.
With only 11 reference pathogens (10 available in each LOPO fold), the 13-dimensional pathogen profile space is severely undersampled (dimensions-to-samples ratio $\approx 1.1$), making $k$NN matching unreliable and introducing noise rather than adaptation.

However, the oracle strategy (mean MCC = 0.160) approximately doubles EqualWeight performance (0.083), confirming that per-pathogen weighting provides genuine improvement when labeled data is available.
Closing this gap without labels requires expanding the reference database to 20--30+ pathogens for meaningful $k$NN matching in the pathogen profile space.
This is a finding about the current state of available data, not a failure of the adaptive concept. The oracle ceiling demonstrates that adaptive improvement is possible in principle, but the benchmark currently supports adaptive weighting only as a future algorithmic direction rather than as a validated deployment method.

\paragraph{PAHD-R redesign audit.}
\phantomsection\label{para:pahd_r_redesign_audit}%
A follow-up PAHD-R redesign tested whether the negative kNN-weighting result was a failure of that specific learner rather than of adaptive evidence fusion in general.
PAHD-R is a shared evidence-fusion framework, not a separate algorithm per virus: it defines three global modes---\textbf{Core} (no-AI exact/top-$k$ baseline), \textbf{Augmented} (balanced PLM/structure-assisted prioritization), and \textbf{Review} (low-FPR triage)---and permits repair layers only after biological-prior, coordinate, decoy, and permutation audits.
Core, Augmented, and Review achieved exact MCC values of 0.1689, 0.1680, and 0.1666; $\pm10$ MCC values of 0.5866, 0.6079, and 0.6366; Precision@20 values of 0.2111, 0.1944, and 0.1833; and constrained FPR values of 0.1108, 0.1128, and 0.0696.
All three passed the pooled permutation audit, but region MCC is reported only with exact MCC, Precision@20, constrained FPR, and coverage because broad windows can inflate region-level scores.

The additional calibrated, shrinkage, virus-aware, and selective-callability sweeps improved individual axes but did not replace the three global modes.
Core-Calibrated improved exact MCC to 0.1770 and FPR to 0.0947, shrinkage reduced FPR to 0.0782, the best virus-aware hybrid reached exact MCC 0.1765/FPR 0.0940, and selective callability reached callable-only exact MCC 0.2742, $\pm10$ MCC 0.7276, Precision@20 0.3500, and FPR 0.0354 while accepting only 4 of 9 pathogens.
These results confirm plausible adaptive extensions but remain future-validation candidates because they do not simultaneously satisfy per-pathogen null support, non-abstaining coverage, and external/time-forward validation.

Repair-layer conclusions were similarly bounded: HCV E2 coordinate reconciliation is adopted as an optional repair (exact MCC 0.5257, FPR 0.0000), whereas SARS-CoV-2 RBD/ACE2 and MERS DPP4/contact repairs remain candidates and RSV remains unrepaired.
Thus PAHD-R is best read as evidence that pathogen-aware adaptive fusion is feasible under strict audit boundaries, not as a completed universal adaptive predictor.

\subsection{Information-Type Analysis Summary}
\label{subsec:info_analysis_summary}

The multi-source information integration analysis yields four key findings:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Feature heterogeneity}: The most discriminative information type differs across pathogens (5~distinct best features among 11 pathogens), providing a mechanistic explanation for the scoring$\,\times\,$pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$) observed in the ANOVA.
\item \textbf{Compact core}: A fixed 4-feature core (homoplasy, pLDDT, entropy, frequency) is statistically indistinguishable from the full 10-feature ensemble under nested-LOPO (paired delta $+0.011$, 95\% CI [$-0.018$, $+0.042$], sign-flip permutation $p = 0.61$; Table~\ref{tab:nested_lopo_4core}), while features that are noise on average (ESM-2, SASA) are the best discriminators for specific pathogens.
\item \textbf{Practical search space reduction}: Equal-weight 10-feature integration achieves statistically significant H3N2 vaccine escape enrichment (4.012$\times$, $p = 0.0023$) where single-feature approaches fail to reach significance, transforming exhaustive experimental screening into guided investigation.
\item \textbf{Adaptive weighting potential}: The oracle ceiling (MCC = 0.160 vs.\ EqualWeight 0.083) shows that per-pathogen feature weighting could double performance with labels, but a deployable adaptive algorithm requires expanding the reference database beyond the current 11 pathogens.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Chapter summary.}
This chapter reported the experimental results of MutBench in five sections.
Stage~1 validated the framework on SARS-CoV-2: MutClust-Hybrid achieved hotspot-score 0.778 on synthetic data, though real GISAID data showed F1 = 0.123 while maintaining biological validity (enrichment $>$ 6.9-fold).
Stage~2 extended evaluation to all 11 pathogens (8,580 evaluations), revealing that the scoring $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.296$; 11-pathogen cluster-bootstrap 95\% interval [0.195, 0.333]) is the largest modeled variance component (within-cell residual $\omega^2 \approx 0.39$), with LOPO cross-validation yielding 0/11 exact matches and a 0.265 MCC oracle-vs-generalized gap, and Friedman test showing no reliably superior combination ($p = 0.990$).
The information-type analysis identified a 4-feature core (homoplasy, pLDDT, entropy, frequency) that is statistically indistinguishable from the full 10-feature ensemble under nested-LOPO (paired delta $+0.011$, $p = 0.61$; Table~\ref{tab:nested_lopo_4core}). Vaccine escape cross-validation was carried primarily by HIV-1 (7.19$\times$, 82\% of escape set disjoint from Layer~A; Bonferroni-adjusted $p = 2.5 \times 10^{-16}$); H3N2's 9.36$\times$ is largely a self-consistency check (69\% Layer~A overlap; novel-only enrichment 7.19$\times$, $p = 0.132$), and SARS-CoV-2 is exploratory after multiple-comparison correction.
The broader implications for adaptive method selection, including knowledge base scaling requirements and future extensions, are discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.
