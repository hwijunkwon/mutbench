\chapter{Experimental Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the experimental results of the MutBench framework presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:mutbench}.
Stage~1 (Sections~\ref{sec:variant_comparison}--\ref{sec:phylo_simulation}) conducts in-depth analysis on SARS-CoV-2, comparing five MutClust variants and two baseline methods through synthetic benchmark, sensitivity analysis, temporal generalization, multi-ground truth evaluation, and additional validation experiments.
Stage~2 (Section~\ref{subsec:9pathogen_scale} onwards) extends the evaluation to 9 RNA viruses with 9 scoring methods $\times$ 41 detection methods, establishing the statistical evidence for pathogen-adaptive method selection.

The results reveal a clear precision--recall trade-off among detection strategies.
MutClust variants using CCM-based seed detection (Original, Fixed, dNdS) achieve near-perfect precision ($> 0.98$) but moderate recall (0.50--0.51), reflecting that CCM detection primarily identifies high-signal hotspot regions (RBD, furin cleavage) while missing low-signal functional sites (NTD supersite, HR1/HR2).
The bug fix expanded the detected positions by 4 while maintaining precision (641 vs. 637).

MutClust-Hybrid achieved the highest F1 (0.785) by combining CCM seed detection with HDBSCAN boundary determination.
Its precision (0.968) approached that of CCM-only variants while recall (0.659) was substantially improved, capturing additional functional regions including parts of the NTD supersite through HDBSCAN's broader boundary estimation.

HDBSCAN-Auto achieved the highest recall (0.944) but exhibited low precision (0.079), detecting 14,916 positions---approximately half the genome---in 21 clusters.
This shows that without CCM seed detection constraining the candidate space, HDBSCAN captures considerable background noise along with actual functional sites.
This demonstrates that domain-specific prior knowledge substantially improves detection specificity.

\textbf{External baseline comparison.}
Both baselines achieved high recall (0.965, 0.974) but low precision (0.404, 0.157), with F1 scores (0.569, 0.270) below all MutClust variants (0.668--0.785).
These results confirm that MutClust's CCM-based adaptive density estimation provides substantial performance improvement over simple threshold- and window-based approaches.

HDBSCAN-Auto achieved high recall (0.944) and stability (0.788) but the lowest hotspot-score (0.604) due to low precision (0.079), illustrating the value of composite metrics over single-metric evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:mutbench_method_comparison}).

\textbf{Two-stage pipeline validation.}
To combine the high recall of HDBSCAN-Auto with the high precision of MutClust-Hybrid, a two-stage detection pipeline was additionally validated.
The intersection of positions detected by HDBSCAN-Auto (14,916 positions) and MutClust-Hybrid (852 positions) yielded 831 positions in 6 clusters, maintaining precision of 0.968 while improving stability to 0.740, achieving the highest hotspot-score (0.784), with F1 (0.772) approaching that of MutClust-Hybrid alone (0.785) (Table~\ref{tab:twostep_pipeline}).
In contrast, the union (14,964 positions) achieved the highest recall (0.970) and stability (0.794) but suffered a sharp drop in precision to 0.081.
These results demonstrate that the complementarity of the two methods is most effectively realized through the intersection strategy, providing a basis for strategy selection according to application context (discussed in detail in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}).

\subsection{Multi-Ground Truth Evaluation}
\label{sec:multi_gt}

No single ground truth can capture all dimensions of mutational importance.
To address this, detection methods were evaluated against three complementary ground truths, each measuring a different biological phenomenon:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Functional regions} (439 AA positions, 34.5\% of Spike; this AA-level union of domain annotations includes broader boundary definitions than the nucleotide-level ground truth (1,251 nt, 417 AA after deduplication) used in the synthetic benchmark): Known domain annotations (RBD, NTD supersite, furin cleavage, fusion peptide, HR1, HR2).
\item \textbf{DMS-escape} (252 positions, 19.8\%): Experimentally measured single-mutation cell entry fitness effects~\cite{dadonaite2023dms} (top 20th percentile).
\item \textbf{Convergent evolution} (97 positions, 7.6\%): Literature-based sites under recurrent positive selection across independent SARS-CoV-2 lineages~\cite{cao2023ba286,carabelli2023convergent}. These include RBD immune evasion positions, ACE2 binding enhancement mutations, furin cleavage site adaptations, and S2 convergent substitutions (this expanded set includes all literature-reported convergent evolution positions, compared to the stricter 25-position Layer~A set used in the 9-pathogen benchmark).
\end{itemize}

The three ground truths are largely non-overlapping: the Jaccard similarity between DMS-escape and convergent evolution positions is 0.006, indicating near-complete independence.
This confirms that population frequency, experimental fitness, and evolutionary convergence capture fundamentally different aspects of mutational importance.

Critically, the best-performing method differed depending on the ground truth (Table~\ref{tab:multi_gt}).

For the strict convergent evolution subset (multi-source positions only), TC-freq-v3 achieved the highest enrichment ratio (3.43-fold, F1 = 0.108).

These differing rankings demonstrate that no single detection method is dominant across all biological dimensions of hotspot importance.
This multi-ground truth analysis provides a critical methodological insight: population frequency and experimental fitness are complementary rather than redundant measures of mutational importance.
Benchmarking frameworks that rely on a single ground truth risk systematic bias toward methods optimized for that specific biological dimension, potentially penalizing methods that capture orthogonal aspects of functional importance (Figure~\ref{fig:multi_ground_truth}; Figure~\ref{fig:dms_evaluation}).

\subsection{Real GISAID H-score Benchmark}
\label{sec:real_gisaid_benchmark}

To address circularity concerns with synthetic data, a benchmark was conducted using the actual GISAID 2020--2021 H-score data used in the MutClust paper~\cite{youn2025mutclust} as input.
This data consists of H-scores calculated for each of 1,273 Spike amino acid positions from SARS-CoV-2 sequences collected from January 2020 to December 2021, representing actual observed data rather than synthetic generation.
After mapping to the nucleotide level, only 342 of 29,903 genome positions (1.14\%) had nonzero H-scores, exhibiting a sparse distribution qualitatively different from the uniform background noise of synthetic data.

The results are presented in Table~\ref{tab:real_gisaid}.

Three key differences from the synthetic benchmark were observed.

\textbf{First, substantial decline in absolute performance.}
The F1 of all MutClust variants dropped by 0.54--0.66 compared to the synthetic benchmark (e.g., MutClust-Hybrid: 0.785 $\to$ 0.123).
This is attributable to the sparsity of real data: many of the 1,251 ground truth positions (conserved regions such as HR1, HR2) have H-score = 0, and positions with H-score of 0 cannot be detected by any density-based method.
This result honestly reveals that the synthetic benchmark overestimates actual performance and confirms the methodological limitation that density-based hotspot detection can only detect ``functional sites that mutate'' and is inherently unable to detect ``conserved functional sites.''

\textbf{Second, shift in method rankings.}
While MutClust-Hybrid had the highest hotspot-score (0.778) on synthetic data, it performed nearly identically to MutClust-Fixed (0.386) on real data (0.383).
The advantage of HDBSCAN boundary determination manifests in continuous H-score distributions (synthetic data) but is dominated by CCM seed detection in discrete and sparse distributions (real data).
The sliding window achieving the highest F1 (0.420) indicates that a wide detection window is advantageous for encompassing adjacent functional regions in sparse data.

\textbf{Third, consistently significant enrichment ratios.}
Although absolute F1 values are low, the enrichment ratios of all methods are consistently high at 6.9--8.0-fold (relative to random expectation), confirming that detected positions are significantly enriched in functional regions in both synthetic and real data.
This indicates that the biological validity of H-score-based detection is maintained regardless of data type.

These results reinforce a core methodological lesson of MutBench: the relative superiority of methods depends on the H-score distribution characteristics of the input data---continuous vs. sparse, uniform background vs. discrete background---and conclusions from synthetic benchmarks should not be unconditionally extrapolated to real data.

\subsection{Comparison with External Methods}
\label{sec:external_comparison}

To complement the internal comparison among MutClust variants, two independent clustering-based methods---OPTICS and kernel density estimation (KDE)---were evaluated on the same synthetic benchmark.
OPTICS is a density-based clustering approach similar to DBSCAN, but automatically extracts variable-density clusters through reachability distance (min\_samples=5, $\xi$=0.05).
KDE is a nonparametric approach that detects peaks above the 90th percentile of the H-score kernel density function and then expands adjacent positions into clusters.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:external_methods}, MutClust-Hybrid achieved the highest hotspot-score (0.778) even in the comparison including external independent methods.

\textbf{OPTICS/KDE parameter sensitivity.}
To rule out the influence of parameter settings on external method results, parameter sensitivity analyses were performed on synthetic data for OPTICS (min\_samples $\in \{2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50\}$, $\xi \in \{0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$) and KDE (bandwidth $\in \{1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50\}$, threshold percentile $\in \{70, 80, 85, 90, 95, 99\}$).
Parameter sensitivity analysis across 30 OPTICS and 36 KDE configurations confirmed that MutClust-Hybrid's superiority is attributable to the structural advantage of domain-specific seed detection rather than unfavorable parameter settings.
Even at OPTICS's best parameters (min\_samples=20, $\xi$=0.10, F1=0.707), precision (0.563) remained substantially below MutClust-Hybrid (0.968).
KDE failed to perform effective detection across all tested configurations on both synthetic and real data.

Having established the relative performance of detection methods, the following sections examine structural limitations of the H-score metric itself.

\section{Robustness and Sensitivity Validation}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Statistical Validation}
\label{sec:statistical_validation}

To evaluate the statistical robustness of the benchmark results, bootstrap confidence interval estimation, permutation tests (random label shuffling + circular permutation), pairwise comparison tests, and Fisher's exact test were performed.
All bootstrap confidence intervals reported in this section were estimated using the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) method~\cite{efron1987better} to correct for bias and asymmetry in small samples ($n = 7$).

\textbf{Permutation test.}
In random label shuffling (1,000 iterations), precision and recall of all methods were significant at $p < 0.001$.
In circular permutation tests (200 iterations), the observed F1 of all 5 methods substantially exceeded the null distribution:
MutClust-Hybrid (F1 = 0.785, null mean = 0.023, $z$ = 9.97, $p < 0.005$),
MutClust-Original (F1 = 0.668, null mean = 0.018, $z$ = 9.25, $p < 0.005$),
MutClust-Fixed (F1 = 0.669, null mean = 0.018, $z$ = 9.25, $p < 0.005$),
HDBSCAN-Auto (F1 = 0.146, null mean = 0.076, $z$ = 11.31, $p < 0.005$).
The significance of all methods even under circular permutation, which preserves the spatial autocorrelation of H-scores, confirms that the spatial overlap between detected hotspots and functional regions is a genuine biological signal rather than a byproduct of H-score cluster structure.

\textbf{Hotspot-score null distribution.}
To validate the statistical significance of the composite hotspot-score metric itself, the null distribution of hotspot-scores was estimated through circular permutation (200 iterations).
The observed hotspot-scores of all 5 methods significantly exceeded the null distribution:
MutClust-Hybrid (HS = 0.778, null mean = 0.251, $z$ = 9.97, $p < 0.005$),
MutClust-Fixed (HS = 0.646, null mean = 0.162, $z$ = 9.25, $p < 0.005$),
MutClust-Original (HS = 0.638, null mean = 0.153, $z$ = 9.25, $p < 0.005$),
HDBSCAN-Auto (HS = 0.604, null mean = 0.441, $z$ = 11.31, $p < 0.005$).
These results confirm that among the three components of the hotspot-score (recall, precision, stability), recall and precision---which depend on spatial overlap---cannot be achieved at the observed levels under random placement, demonstrating that the observed values of the composite metric are not attributable to chance.

\textbf{Fisher's exact test.}
The statistical significance of enrichment ratios reported in the multi-ground truth evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:multi_gt}) was validated using Fisher's exact test.
After applying Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction ($q < 0.05$) to 128 comparisons, 3 comparisons were significant:
functional region enrichment of TC-freq-v2 (1.16-fold, $p_{\text{adj}}$ = 0.002),
functional region enrichment of TC-freq-v3 (1.59-fold, $p_{\text{adj}}$ = 0.023),
convergent evolution enrichment of CH-score (1.70-fold, $p_{\text{adj}}$ = 0.023).
Additionally, 2 comparisons were significant at $q < 0.10$ (CH-score convergent evolution top10pct, E-score convergent evolution top20pct).
The enrichment of TC-freq-v3 in the strict convergent evolution subset (3.43-fold, $p$ = 0.013, $p_{\text{adj}}$ = 0.161) and the convergent evolution enrichment of saturated H-score (1.85-fold, $p$ = 0.048, $p_{\text{adj}}$ = 0.269) did not reach significance after BH FDR correction; however, the enrichment trend at convergent evolution positions was consistently observed across multiple approaches (CH-score, E-score, TC-freq-v3).

\textbf{BCa bootstrap confidence intervals.}
With a small sample of $n = 7$ functional regions, percentile confidence intervals may not adequately reflect bias and asymmetry.
To correct for this, bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals were applied.
The BCa method adjusts confidence interval percentiles using the bias correction factor $z_0$ (median bias of the bootstrap distribution) and the acceleration factor $a$ (skewness correction based on jackknife)~\cite{efron1987better}.
By resampling the 7 functional regions with replacement (1,000 iterations) and computing region-level recall, precision, and stability, the BCa 95\% confidence intervals for hotspot-score are presented in Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap_ci}.

\textbf{Power analysis.}
The statistical power for detecting differences between methods with an effective sample size of $n = 7$ functional regions was evaluated post hoc.
In a paired design with significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ and $n = 7$, power of 0.80 is achieved at an effect size of Cohen's $d \geq 1.4$.
The effect size computed by dividing the observed hotspot-score difference between MutClust-Hybrid and MutClust-Fixed (0.778 -- 0.646 = 0.132) by the bootstrap standard error is $d \approx 2.1$, confirming that this pairwise comparison has sufficient power.
However, the small difference between MutClust-Fixed and MutClust-Original (0.646 -- 0.638 = 0.008, $d \approx 0.15$) has extremely low detection power at $n = 7$, requiring caution in claiming equivalence of these two methods.

\textbf{Hotspot-score weight sensitivity.}
To verify the robustness of the equal weight ($1/3$) setting for hotspot-score, method rankings were analyzed across 171 weight combinations ($w_r + w_p + w_s = 1$, step size 0.05) (Table~\ref{tab:weight_sensitivity}).
MutClust-Hybrid maintained first rank in \textbf{71.9\%} (123/171) of the entire weight space, while HDBSCAN-Auto ranked first in 28.1\% (48/171).
Rank switching occurred only in extreme recall-oriented regions where recall weight $\geq 0.60$ and precision weight $\leq 0.20$.

First, MutClust-Hybrid's first-place ranking is robust to weight selection, confirming that the equal weight setting is not an artificial result of a particular weight bias.
Second, for early warning purposes (recall $\geq 0.60$), HDBSCAN-Auto may be more suitable, demonstrating that the MutBench framework is a tool that \textbf{supports method selection according to application context} rather than designating a single ``best-performing'' method.

Pairwise comparisons confirmed that MutClust-Hybrid achieved significantly higher hotspot-scores than all other methods (all comparisons $p < 0.001$, score difference range: 0.132 relative to MutClust-Fixed to 0.174 relative to HDBSCAN-Auto).
These results establish that the performance rankings in Table~\ref{tab:hotspot_scores} are robust to ground truth sampling uncertainty and are not attributable to chance.

\subsection{Temporal Generalization (GISAID 2024--2025)}
\label{sec:temporal}

To evaluate whether MutBench results generalize to temporally distinct data, 9,957 full-length SARS-CoV-2 genome sequences (host: human) collected from January 2024 to December 2025 from GISAID were analyzed, with weekly subsampling for temporal representativeness.

The temporal validation revealed a critical phenomenon: \textbf{H-score saturation}.
The original 2020--2021 dataset produced nonzero H-scores at 114 positions (standard deviation = 1.88), whereas the 2024--2025 dataset produced nonzero H-scores at all 1,273 Spike positions (mean = 8.47, standard deviation = 0.098).
This nearly uniform distribution eliminates the discriminative power of H-scores, as there is virtually no contrast between positions.

Consequently, all methods exhibited a precision of approximately 0.33, nearly identical to the ground truth base rate (1,251 / 3,819 = 0.328, where 3,819 = 1,273 coding AA $\times$ 3 nt, excluding the stop codon).
MutClust-Fixed and MutClust-dNdS detected all 3,819 positions as a single cluster (precision = 0.328, recall = 1.0), while MutClust-Hybrid maintained some selectivity (3,225 positions, 377 clusters) but showed similar results without meaningful improvement in precision (Table~\ref{tab:temporal}).

\subsection{Indel Exclusion Impact}
\label{sec:indel_impact}

H-score is a fixed-coordinate metric that measures point mutation frequency at each position relative to a reference sequence and therefore cannot capture insertions or deletions (indels).
Analysis of 9,957 GISAID 2024--2025 Spike sequences revealed that 99.8\% carry at least one functional indel (e.g., del69-70, Y144del, del156-157), yet all indel positions have H-score = 0.000, confirming complete exclusion from H-score-based detection.
Among approximately 35 major Spike functional variants, at least 5 (approximately 14\%) are indels.

To quantify the detection impact, an extended ground truth was constructed by adding 5 indel positions (27 nt) to the 1,251 nt ground truth.
A synthetic experiment comparing the original H-score with an augmented H-score (functional signals injected at indel positions) showed that the F1 impact for MutClust-Hybrid was negligible ($\Delta$F1 = $-$0.002), because the 27 additional positions constitute only 2.1\% of the extended ground truth.
While the quantitative impact on F1 is small, indel exclusion represents a structural limitation of the fixed-coordinate framework, as biologically important structural variants are systematically invisible to the scoring function.
The implications for the MutBench framework are discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.

\subsection{3D Structure Analysis}

To validate the structural plausibility of linear sequence-based hotspot detection, the 3D spatial distribution of detected hotspot positions was analyzed using C$\alpha$ coordinates from PDB 6VSB (Spike protein prefusion structure, Wrapp et al. 2020).

\subsection{Phylogenetic Non-Independence Simulation}
\label{sec:phylo_simulation}

To quantitatively evaluate whether H-scores can be biased by phylogenetic structure, coalescent-based simulations were performed.

\subsubsection{Proof of Concept}
\label{subsec:phylo_correction_poc}

In a coalescent simulation (100 replicates, $N = 500$), a single-origin ``founder mutation'' (frequency 90\%, D614G-like) and a ``convergent mutation'' arising independently 15 times (E484K-like) were placed alongside 50 neutral mutations on single branches.
H-score ranked the founder mutation above convergent mutations in 100\% of replicates, whereas Fitch parsimony-based homoplasy counts correctly ranked convergent mutations at the top in 100\%.
The Spearman rank correlation between H-score and number of independent origins was $\rho = -0.876$ ($p = 1.28 \times 10^{-64}$), quantitatively demonstrating that \textbf{frequency-based scores systematically conflate founder effects with positive selection}.

In the MutBench ground truth, D614G (position 614) is deliberately excluded from Layer~A (convergent evolution) and Layer~B (purifying selection), because it reached fixation via a single founder event~\cite{korber2020tracking} rather than through recurrent independent emergence.
These results provide a conceptual demonstration that phylogeny-based independent origin count estimation can effectively correct the founder effect bias.
However, this simulation assumes ideal conditions ($N = 500$, fully resolved tree); at GISAID scale ($> 16$M sequences), polytomies, computational cost, and recombination events introduce additional challenges.

\subsubsection{TreeTime ML Ancestral Reconstruction Validation}
\label{subsec:treetime_ml_validation}

An extended validation using TreeTime~\cite{sagulenko2018treetime} ML-based ancestral reconstruction ($N = 2{,}000$, 50 replicates) confirmed that both Fitch parsimony and TreeTime-ML achieved AUC = 1.000 for discriminating convergent from non-convergent mutations, while H-score achieved AUC = 0.000.
The 100\% discrimination accuracy was maintained when scaling from $N = 500$ to $N = 2{,}000$.

\subsubsection{TreeTime Ancestral Reconstruction Validation on Real GISAID Sequences}
\label{subsec:treetime_gisaid_validation}

To validate beyond synthetic simulations, 500 GISAID sequences (2024--2025) were analyzed with TreeTime ML ancestral reconstruction.
The Spearman rank correlation across 1,273 Spike positions was $\rho = -0.107$ ($p = 1.36 \times 10^{-4}$), revealing a \textbf{weak negative correlation} between H-score and homoplasy score.
This demonstrates that the two scoring systems capture fundamentally different evolutionary signals: H-score reflects frequency relative to the reference, while homoplasy reflects independent recurrent occurrence.
Key positions illustrate the divergence: P681 ranks 2nd by H-score but 1,134th by homoplasy (founder effect), while L452 and K417 have H-score = 0 but homoplasy of 91 (convergent evolution).
These results confirm that the founder effect bias of H-score predicted in simulations is observed in real GISAID sequences.

\textbf{Key finding}: The detection family $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.285$) is the \textbf{largest source of variance, exceeding all main effects and other interactions}. Under Cohen's effect size benchmarks~\cite{cohen1988statistical}, $\omega^2 > 0.14$ constitutes a large effect, confirming that this interaction is substantively large.
This means that the best-performing detection method varies systematically depending on the pathogen, and no single pathogen-independent best method exists.
The pathogen main effect ($\omega^2 = 0.260$) is also large, reflecting that intrinsic differences in mutation structure across pathogens (entropy distribution shape, density of positively selected sites, etc.) determine the performance level itself.

In contrast, the main effects of scoring and detection family are small at $\omega^2 = 0.027$ and $0.025$, respectively, confirming that ``which method is applied to which pathogen'' is the dominant factor determining performance, rather than the scoring or detection method itself.

The three-way interaction (score $\times$ family $\times$ pathogen) was not reported in this analysis.
The degrees of freedom for this term are $(9-1) \times (15-1) \times (9-1) = 896$, and after subtracting the degrees of freedom for main effects and two-way interactions from the total number of observations ($n = 2{,}544$), the residual degrees of freedom are insufficient. Moreover, due to the unbalanced design (number of valid detection methods per pathogen: 33--38), many cells are empty, making stable estimation of the three-way interaction infeasible.
Since the family $\times$ pathogen two-way interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.285$) has already been identified as the largest source of variance, the omission of the three-way interaction does not affect the core conclusions.

\textbf{LOPO Cross-Validation.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_lopo}
LOPO (Leave-One-Pathogen-Out) cross-validation was performed to evaluate whether the best combination selected from 8 training pathogens is also best-performing for the excluded test pathogen.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Combination match rate}: 0/9 (0\%). In all 9 iterations, the training-best combination and the test oracle combination were mismatched.
\item \textbf{Mean generalization ratio}: 4.9\%. The test MCC of the training-best combination was only 4.9\% of the oracle MCC on average.
\item \textbf{Mean performance gap}: 0.277. The average MCC difference between the training-best combination and the oracle was 0.277, representing a substantial loss of oracle performance.
\item \textbf{Most frequent training-best combination}: E\_only + SWAN was selected in 8/9 iterations, an artifact of SWAN producing valid results only for Norovirus (MCC = 0.311) and MCC = 0.000 elsewhere.
\end{itemize}

These results demonstrate that the strategy of selecting a method that is ``on average good across multiple pathogens'' fails to predict performance for a specific pathogen, strongly supporting the need for pathogen-specific adaptive method selection.
When LOPO is performed excluding the SWAN family (9 variants)\footnote{Excluding SWAN reduces the number of evaluations from 2,544 to 2,414 (a decrease of 130), and the training-best combination changes primarily to P*E + Wavelet($t$ = 1.5). The mean generalization ratio improves from 4.9\% to 11.6\%, and the mean performance gap decreases from 0.277 to 0.239; however, the combination match rate of 0/9 is maintained, leaving the core conclusion unchanged.}, the combination match rate of 0/9 is maintained, confirming that the core conclusion of ``non-transferability of best methods across pathogens'' remains unchanged.

The 0/9 combination match rate and 4.9\% generalization ratio are not limitations of the benchmark but rather \textbf{key findings}. These results quantitatively demonstrate that the premise of a ``single best-performing method'' itself does not hold, providing the strongest statistical evidence for the necessity of pathogen-adaptive method selection (PAHD). $n=9$ covers the major representative RNA virus pathogens, and despite the sample size constraint, the statistical significance of inter-pathogen heterogeneity was confirmed by four independent lines of evidence: ANOVA interaction dominance ($\omega^2 = 0.285$), unique best combinations (9/9), Friedman test ($p = 0.0015$), and LOPO cross-validation (0/9 match).

\textbf{Friedman Test.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_friedman}
Using the 9 pathogens as blocks, a Friedman test was performed on the performance differences among the top 20 scoring--detection combinations.
$k = 20$ corresponds to approximately the top 7\% of the total valid combinations (247--305 per pathogen) and represents the competitive combination group while maintaining a treatment count that is not excessive relative to the number of blocks ($b = 9$)\footnote{To verify the robustness of the $k$ selection, the Friedman test was also performed at $k = 10$, $k = 30$, and $k = 50$. $k = 10$: $\chi^2 = 22.1$, $p = 0.009$; $k = 30$: $\chi^2 = 58.7$, $p = 0.001$; $k = 50$: $\chi^2 = 87.3$, $p < 0.001$. All $k$ values yielded $p < 0.01$, confirming that the significance of performance differences among top combinations is robust to the choice of $k$.}.

\begin{equation}
\chi^2 = 42.44, \quad p = 0.0015
\end{equation}

With $p = 0.0015 < 0.01$, the result is statistically significant, confirming that significant performance differences exist among the top 20 combinations.
This rejects the null hypothesis that ``all top combinations are equivalent and any can be used interchangeably,'' demonstrating that the choice of combination has a substantial impact on performance.

\textbf{Pathogen-Level BCa Bootstrap Confidence Intervals.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_bootstrap}
The best single combination across all pathogens was evaluated using BCa bootstrap.
The selection criterion was the combination with the highest mean MCC among those whose 95\% BCa confidence interval lower bound exceeds 0.
Ranking by mean MCC alone could place combinations that perform well only on specific pathogens at the top (e.g., E\_only + SWAN achieves MCC = 0.311 on Norovirus but MCC = 0.000 on the remaining 8 pathogens); thus, the condition that the confidence interval excludes 0 ensures consistent performance across all pathogens.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Best single combination}: P*E + Wavelet ($t$ = 1.5)
\item \textbf{Mean MCC}: 0.124
\item \textbf{95\% BCa confidence interval}: [0.047, 0.202]
\end{itemize}

The width of the confidence interval (0.155) is wide, reflecting high variability in performance across the 9 pathogens.
The mean MCC of the best single combination (0.124) is only 42\% of the mean oracle MCC across pathogens (0.298), quantitatively confirming that a single fixed combination substantially falls short of oracle performance for more than half of all pathogens.

Although this MCC value appears low, it should be interpreted in the context of the following considerations.
(1)~This is the 9-pathogen average of a single fixed combination and corresponds to 42\% of the per-pathogen oracle MCC (mean 0.298). Using pathogen-specific best combinations improves MCC to the range of 0.088--0.549.
(2)~Compared to the random baseline (MCC mean=0.001, standard deviation=0.003), the observed MCC=0.124 exceeds the random expectation by approximately 41 standard deviations. This confirms unambiguous statistical significance regardless of distributional assumptions.
(3)~Hotspot detection is inherently an imbalanced binary classification (positive rate approximately 1--16\%), and MCC $>$ 0.1 in such extreme imbalance is not trivial.

\textbf{Subsampling Robustness.}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_subsampling}
The benchmark was repeated at MSA sequence proportions of 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, and 100\%, and the overall pathogen-average MCC was stable in the range of 0.054--0.058.
The MCC variation with decreasing sample size was within $\pm$0.002, confirming that the benchmark results are robust to sequence sample size.
This supports the conclusion that the benchmark findings reflect intrinsic properties of pathogen mutation structures rather than artifacts of a particular sample size.

\textbf{Cross-Paradigm Validation: Protein Language Model Scoring.}
\label{subsec:esm_validation}
To test whether the benchmark findings generalize beyond MSA-derived scoring formulas, an independent scoring paradigm was evaluated: the ESM-2 protein language model (650M parameters; Lin et al., 2023). ESM-2 computes per-position log-likelihood ratios (ESM-LLR) from a single consensus sequence using learned evolutionary representations, requiring no multiple sequence alignment. This provides orthogonal validation of the benchmark framework because the scoring signal originates from a fundamentally different methodology---a deep neural network trained on millions of protein families---rather than from the same MSA used by the nine existing scoring formulas.

ESM-LLR was computed for all nine pathogens using wild-type marginal scoring: a single forward pass through the model yields $\text{ESM-LLR}[i] = -\log P(a_i \mid \text{context})$, where $a_i$ is the consensus amino acid at position $i$. \subsection{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{subsec:pahd_motivation_summary}

Synthesizing the key results of the 9-pathogen benchmark, the need for pathogen-adaptive method selection is demonstrated by four independent lines of evidence.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Interaction dominance}: The detection family $\times$ pathogen interaction ($\omega^2 = 0.285$) exceeds all main effects. The best-performing method depends on the pathogen.
\item \textbf{Unique best combinations}: 9 unique best combinations were observed across 9/9 pathogens. No two pathogens share the same best combination.
\item \textbf{Generalization failure}: LOPO cross-validation yielded 0/9 combination matches with a mean generalization ratio of 4.9\%. The best combination selected from training pathogens does not generalize to new pathogens.
\item \textbf{Significant combination differences}: Friedman $\chi^2 = 42.44$, $p = 0.0015$. The choice of combination has a significant impact on performance.
\end{enumerate}

These results statistically establish that ``the best-performing method differs for each pathogen,'' motivating the conceptual PAHD framework---a future system that would adaptively select the best combination by profiling the mutation characteristics of each pathogen.

The following analysis applies the 9-pathogen benchmark results as a knowledge base for a proof-of-concept method selection experiment, demonstrating how MutBench's findings can be translated into practical method recommendations.

\subsection{PAHD Prototype Validation}
