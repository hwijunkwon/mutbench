\section{Viral Mutation Hotspot Detection}
\label{sec:bg_hotspot}

\subsection{Mutation Hotspot Detection Methods}
\label{subsec:bg_hotspot_methods}

Mutation hotspots are regions within a genome where mutations are concentrated in a non-uniform manner, likely reflecting functionally important sites under structural or immunological selective pressure.
Identifying hotspots in viral genomes is critical for predicting variant emergence, selecting vaccine targets, and developing therapeutics~\cite{holmes2009evolution}.
Existing hotspot detection approaches can be categorized into frequency-based, entropy-based, and density-based methods.

\textbf{Frequency-based approaches} calculate mutation frequency at the amino acid or nucleotide level and designate positions with high frequency as hotspots.
While intuitive and easy to implement, they have two fundamental limitations.
First, \textbf{lineage bias}: when a specific lineage is overrepresented in the database, its defining mutations receive disproportionately high frequency scores.
Mutations such as D614G, observed in nearly all modern SARS-CoV-2 sequences, reflect genuine fitness advantages, but the rate of near-fixation is amplified by founder effects during the early pandemic, making it difficult to distinguish the relative contributions of selection and genetic drift from frequency data alone.
Second, \textbf{neglect of rare variants}: mutations that occur at low frequency but exhibit diverse forms---potentially indicating immune evasion or viral adaptation---are systematically overlooked.

\textbf{Entropy-based approaches} use Shannon entropy to measure nucleotide diversity at a given position.
Mullick et al.~\cite{mullick2021entropy} combined Shannon entropy with K-means clustering to detect hotspots in the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein.
However, conventional nucleotide entropy reflects the distribution of all nucleotides regardless of whether mutation has occurred, failing to capture effective diversity conditioned on the occurrence of mutation.
Furthermore, K-means clustering requires the number of clusters to be specified in advance and does not account for density structure.

\textbf{Cancer genomics methods.}
In cancer genomics, various methods have been developed to detect somatic mutation hotspots.
Cancer genomics tools such as MutSigCV~\cite{lawrence2013mutsigcv} (gene-level driver detection), OncodriveCLUST~\cite{tamborero2013oncodriveclust}, HotMAPS~\cite{tokheim2016hotmaps}, and MutSpot~\cite{munoz2020mutspot} target somatic mutations in tumor genomes, which are more than 1,000 times larger than viral genomes and involve fundamentally different mutation dynamics and selective pressures, making direct transfer infeasible.

An important limitation of frequency-based mutation analysis is \textbf{phylogenetic non-independence}: mutations observed in closely related sequences are not independent events~\cite{felsenstein1985phylogenies}.
Phylogeny-aware methods such as TreeTime~\cite{sagulenko2018treetime}, BEAST~\cite{suchard2018beast}, and HyPhy~\cite{kosakovsky2020hyphy} can address this but require high-quality phylogenetic trees and have limited reliability in densely sampled datasets~\cite{turakhia2020ultrafast}.

Simpler approaches to hotspot identification include sliding window methods, which scan fixed-length windows across the genome and flag those exceeding a mutation count threshold, and signal processing techniques such as wavelet transforms, which decompose mutation frequency profiles to identify localized peaks at multiple scales.

\subsection{Density-Based Clustering}
\label{subsec:bg_density_clustering}

DBSCAN~\cite{ester1996dbscan} is a representative density-based clustering algorithm that defines high-density regions as clusters and classifies low-density regions as noise.
It uses two key parameters---epsilon (search radius) and MinPts (minimum number of points)---to define density conditions.
However, DBSCAN uses a \textbf{fixed global epsilon}, making it unable to form appropriate clusters in data with substantial density variation.
This problem manifests in extreme form in viral genomes:
direct application to the SARS-CoV-2 genome with default parameters results in 99.76\% of the data being merged into a single massive cluster; even with manually tuned parameters ($\varepsilon = 0.15$, MinPts $= 5$), only 4 undifferentiated clusters are obtained, rendering biologically meaningful hotspot distinction impossible~\cite{youn2025mutclust}.

HDBSCAN~\cite{campello2013hdbscan} addresses the fundamental limitation of DBSCAN's fixed epsilon.
It hierarchically explores DBSCAN results across various epsilon values to adaptively extract clusters in response to density variation.
It operates with a single parameter, min\_cluster\_size, without requiring explicit specification of epsilon.
It can detect clusters of arbitrary shape, automatically identifies noise points, and provides probabilistic interpretation of cluster membership.

OPTICS~\cite{ankerst1999optics} generates a reachability plot revealing clustering structure at all density levels but requires post-processing for cluster extraction.

\subsection{Deep Mutational Scanning as Ground Truth}
\label{subsec:bg_dms}

\textbf{Deep Mutational Scanning (DMS)} is an experimental technique that systematically measures the functional effects of all possible single amino acid substitutions within a protein~\cite{fowler2014dms}.
For SARS-CoV-2, Starr et al.~\cite{starr2020dms} measured ACE2 binding affinity and expression in the receptor-binding domain (RBD), and Dadonaite et al.~\cite{dadonaite2023dms} measured pseudovirus cell entry fitness across the full-length Spike protein.
Bloom and Neher~\cite{bloom2023fitness} provided an integrated analysis of DMS fitness effects across all SARS-CoV-2 proteins.
DMS data provides a natural ground truth for hotspot benchmarking: positions where mutations exhibit large fitness effects correspond to biologically functional sites that detection methods should identify~\cite{livesey2020using}. Deep generative models have also been shown to predict the effects of mutations from sequence variation alone~\cite{riesselman2018deep}, providing a complementary computational approach to DMS-based validation.


\subsection{Protein Language Models for Variant Effect Prediction}
\label{subsec:bg_plm}

Protein language models (PLMs) trained on large-scale protein sequence databases have emerged as a complementary computational approach to variant effect prediction.
ESM-2~\cite{lin2023esm2,rives2021biological}, trained on 250 million protein sequences from UniRef, computes per-position log-likelihood ratios that quantify how unexpected a mutation is relative to learned evolutionary constraints, and has been applied to predict variant fitness effects.
ProtTrans~\cite{elnaggar2022prottrans} adopts transformer architectures (including BERT and T5 variants) pretrained on UniRef and BFD databases, achieving competitive performance on protein function prediction tasks.
More recently, ESM-3 extended the ESM family to a multimodal generative model integrating sequence, structure, and function tokens.
Despite the growing adoption of PLMs in protein engineering and variant effect prediction, no systematic comparison of PLM-derived features exists for viral mutation hotspot detection across multiple pathogens.
In this dissertation, ESM-2 log-likelihood ratios and embedding-derived semantic change scores are evaluated as two of the 10 positional features in the MutBench information analysis (Chapter~\ref{ch:adapt}).

\subsection{Viral Genomic Surveillance Platforms}
\label{subsec:bg_surveillance}

Real-time viral genomic surveillance platforms provide the operational context in which hotspot detection methods are deployed, but serve a fundamentally different purpose.

\textbf{Nextstrain}~\cite{nextstrain2018hadfield} integrates phylogenetic analysis with geographic and temporal metadata to track pathogen evolution in real time. Its primary function is to answer ``which lineages are currently spreading and where?''---for example, tracking the geographic expansion of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron sublineages or seasonal influenza clades. Nextstrain visualizes the evolutionary relationships among circulating variants but does not evaluate or compare methods for detecting mutation-prone positions.

\textbf{PANGO}~\cite{rambaut2020pangolin} provides a standardized lineage classification system for SARS-CoV-2, assigning labels (e.g., BA.2.86, JN.1) to sequences based on their phylogenetic placement. Its purpose is taxonomic organization---labeling ``what is this variant?''---rather than identifying which genomic positions are likely to accumulate future mutations of functional significance.

These platforms cannot substitute for hotspot detection benchmarking for three reasons:
(1)~they operate at the \textit{lineage/variant level} (which variant is spreading), not at the \textit{position level} (which genomic position is mutation-prone);
(2)~they are \textit{retrospective} (tracking variants that have already emerged) rather than \textit{prospective} (identifying positions predisposed to future functionally important mutations);
and (3)~they do not compare or evaluate detection algorithms---they are end-user tools, not method evaluation frameworks.
The distinction is critical: surveillance answers ``what has happened?'' while hotspot detection aims to answer ``where might important mutations occur next?''
MutBench addresses the latter question by providing a systematic framework for evaluating which detection methods most effectively identify biologically meaningful mutation-prone positions.

\subsection{Feature Importance Analysis in Viral Mutation Prediction}
\label{subsec:bg_feature_importance}

Several recent studies have analyzed which biological features predict viral mutation patterns, providing context for the multi-source information analysis in this dissertation (Chapter~\ref{ch:adapt}).
Maher et al.~\cite{maher2022predicting} analyzed features predicting future SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern, finding that epidemiological features (geographic spread, growth rate) dominated over sequence-based features for a single pathogen.
Rodriguez-Rivas et al.~\cite{rodriguez2022epistatic} demonstrated that epistatic coupling models (DCA-based) achieve AUC = 0.76 for predicting escape mutations, outperforming non-epistatic models (AUC = 0.63), highlighting the value of co-evolutionary information.
Hie et al.~\cite{hie2021learning} showed that protein language model embeddings capture ``semantic change'' that predicts viral escape, providing a complementary deep learning perspective.
However, these studies are limited to single pathogens (primarily SARS-CoV-2 or influenza) and do not systematically compare feature importance \textit{across} multiple pathogens---a gap that the present study addresses.

\subsection{Algorithm Selection and Benchmarking Methodology}
\label{subsec:bg_algorithm_selection}

The finding that the best-performing detection method depends on the pathogen (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}) is an instance of Rice's algorithm selection problem~\cite{rice1976algorithm}: given a problem instance (pathogen) with specific characteristics, which algorithm should be applied?
This framework has been formalized in the machine learning community through meta-learning~\cite{kerschke2019algorithm,bischl2016aslib} and automated algorithm selection (AutoML)~\cite{feurer2015automl,kotthoff2016algorithm}, but has not been applied to viral genomics.
The benchmarking methodology adopted in this dissertation follows established principles from computational biology: Weber et al.~\cite{weber2019benchmarking} proposed guidelines for neutral benchmarking (independent evaluation, multiple metrics, controlled experimental design), and Boulesteix et al.~\cite{boulesteix2017towards} emphasized the importance of avoiding ``self-assessment bias'' where method developers evaluate their own methods.
MutBench adheres to these principles by evaluating all methods---including the author's own MutClust---under identical conditions with independent ground truth.

\subsection{Benchmarking Gap in Viral Hotspot Detection}
\label{subsec:bg_benchmarking_gap}

The absence of benchmark studies for hotspot detection methods in viral genomics stands in contrast to cancer genomics, where the comprehensive driver gene analysis by Bailey et al.~\cite{bailey2018comprehensive} established a standard benchmark with independent ground truth, enabling systematic comparison of 26 detection methods under uniform conditions.
No analogous benchmark exists for viral mutation hotspot detection: detection methods are evaluated using ad hoc validation against small sets of known functional mutations, without standardized datasets, metrics, or ground truth definitions.
Related efforts in adjacent domains include:
(1)~ProteinGym~\cite{notin2023proteingym}, which benchmarks variant effect predictors using 250+ DMS assays but addresses a per-residue regression task rather than hotspot region detection;
(2)~ConDor~\cite{morel2024condor}, which detects convergent mutations across phylogenies but is a single tool rather than a benchmark framework;
(3)~Pons et al.~\cite{pons2020computational}, who reviewed 40+ cancer hotspot detection methods but produced a descriptive survey without a unified benchmark, and cancer-specific models do not transfer to viral evolutionary dynamics;
and (4)~individual SARS-CoV-2 studies using entropy~\cite{harvey2021tracking} or clustering that validate against small variant sets without standardized ground truth.
Thus, a systematic benchmark with multi-source ground truth, composite evaluation metrics, and cross-pathogen validation remains absent for viral hotspot detection.
This gap is the fundamental motivation for MutBench (Chapter~\ref{ch:mutbench}), the core study of this dissertation.

\section{MutClust: Adaptive Density-Based Hotspot Detection}
\label{sec:mutclust_summary}

\textit{This section is a summary of the paper published in BioData Mining (2025) 18:61~\cite{youn2025mutclust}.}

\subsection{H-score and Adaptive DBSCAN}
\label{subsec:mutclust_hscore}

MutClust is the first density-based algorithm designed for detecting mutation hotspots in viral genomes.
Its core innovations are a novel mutation importance metric called the H-score and an adaptive DBSCAN framework built upon it.

The \textbf{H-score} combines mutation frequency and mutation entropy (diversity conditioned on mutation occurrence) through a logarithmic transformation of their product, thereby simultaneously capturing two complementary signals while suppressing two classes of false positives: lineage-defining mutations with high frequency but low diversity, and noise positions with low frequency but spuriously high diversity.

Built upon the H-score, \textbf{Adaptive DBSCAN} replaces DBSCAN's fixed global epsilon with a neighborhood radius proportional to each position's H-score, assigning wider search radii to biologically significant regions and narrower radii elsewhere.
The algorithm first identifies Core Candidate Mutations (CCMs) that satisfy threshold conditions on individual H-score, neighborhood mean, neighborhood sum, and minimum point count, then expands clusters bidirectionally from each CCM with a diminishing radius.
The key parameters ($\gamma=10$, $d=3$, MinPts$=5$) were empirically determined from 224,318 SARS-CoV-2 sequences, and the overall time complexity is $O(N + M \log M)$ where $N$ is genome length and $M$ is the number of mutation positions.

\subsection{SARS-CoV-2 Hotspot Detection Results}
\label{subsec:mutclust_results_summary}

Analysis of 224,318 GISAID SARS-CoV-2 sequences aligned to the Wuhan-Hu-1 reference genome (NC\_045512.2, 29,903 bp) revealed that MutClust detected \textbf{477} mutation hotspots, compared to only 4 clusters obtained by DBSCAN with tuned parameters---a qualitatively different level of resolution.
Of 10 known functional mutations examined for validation, 9 were contained within hotspots, including D614G, E484K, N501Y, and L452R, and the highest hotspot density was observed in the Spike protein region.
Validation on influenza confirmed partial generalizability but highlighted the need for parameter readjustment across different genome sizes. For H3N2 in particular, Smith et al.~\cite{smith2004antigenic} demonstrated through antigenic cartography that a small number of substitutions in the HA protein drive major antigenic change, underscoring the importance of accurate detection of such key positions.

\subsection{Limitations and Motivation for MutBench Development}
\label{subsec:mutclust_limitations}

Despite the achievements of MutClust, three fundamental limitations serve as the direct motivation for MutBench development.

\textbf{First, implementation-level issues.}
A bug in the diminishing formula of the cluster expansion function causes cumulative rather than linear radius decay, leading to premature termination of cluster expansion and, in certain edge cases, reversal of the decay direction.

\textbf{Second, hardcoded parameters.}
$\gamma=10$, $d=3$, and the CCM thresholds (0.03, 0.01, 0.05) are empirically determined values fitted to 224,318 SARS-CoV-2 sequences.
These values may become inappropriate when the number of sequences or genome size differs, yet the sensitivity of detection results to parameter variation has never been systematically analyzed.
Although applying the same parameters was successful in influenza validation, influenza genomes are approximately one-tenth the size of SARS-CoV-2 ($\sim$2,300 bp/segment vs. $\sim$30,000 bp), and the necessity of parameter readjustment for best performance remains unresolved.

\textbf{Third, the absence of systematic benchmarking.}
MutClust's reliance on self-referential bootstrap validation, without independent ground truth or standardized metrics, exemplifies the broader benchmarking gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:bg_hotspot}.
Quantitative evaluation (precision, recall, stability) based on independent ground truth has not been conducted, and the absence of standardized evaluation metrics makes fair comparison of results across different methods impossible.

Additionally, since H-scores are computed at the nucleotide level, they do not distinguish between synonymous and non-synonymous mutations, necessitating integration with selective pressure indicators such as the dN/dS ratio.
Phylogenetic non-independence---the possibility that mutation frequency is the result of a single founder effect rather than independent positive selection---is also not corrected for.

\vspace{1em}

The core of these limitations is that MutClust proposed a new detection method but failed to simultaneously provide a framework for quantitatively evaluating that method's performance.
This corresponds to the absence of benchmark principles established in cancer genomics~\cite{bailey2018comprehensive} and multi-omics evaluation~\cite{weber2019benchmarking}---neutral evaluation, multiple metrics, and controlled experimental design.
