\chapter{Background and Prior Work}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter presents the background necessary for understanding MutBench, the core research of this dissertation, organized into four sections.

First, prior work on multi-omics integration (Section~\ref{sec:bg_moi}) and viral mutation hotspot detection (Section~\ref{sec:bg_hotspot}) is reviewed.
These two fields represent the domains addressed by the core research of this dissertation (MutBench) and its prior work (MOSD, MutClust), sharing a common reliance on clustering-based methods and a lack of systematic benchmarking.

Subsequently, the two previously published studies that serve as precursors to this dissertation---MOSD (Section~\ref{sec:mosd_summary}) and MutClust (Section~\ref{sec:mutclust_summary})---are summarized.
MOSD established a systematic benchmark methodology for multi-omics integration, and MutClust developed an adaptive algorithm for viral mutation hotspot detection.
This chapter presents both the achievements and limitations of these two studies, clarifying the motivation and methodological foundation for MutBench development.

\section{Multi-Omics Integration}
\label{sec:bg_moi}


Multi-Omics Integration (MOI) combines multi-layered biological data---such as gene expression, DNA methylation, and copy number variation---to achieve comprehensive molecular characterization of complex diseases.
Numerous MOI methods have been developed, including graph-based approaches (SNF~\cite{wang2014snf}, NEMO~\cite{rappoport2018nemo}), matrix factorization methods (MOFA~\cite{argelaguet2018mofa}, iClusterPlus~\cite{shen2013iclusterplus}), and consensus-based approaches (COCA~\cite{hoadley2014coca}, PINSPlus~\cite{nguyen2017pinsplus}).
However, these methods faced the fundamental question of which method performs best under which conditions, and existing benchmark studies~\cite{chauvel2020sample, rappoport2018benchmark, pierrejean2020feature, tini2019multi, duan2021evaluation} each examined only 1--3 factors independently, failing to provide integrated guidelines.
The author previously developed the MOSD framework~\cite{han2025mosd} to address this gap, systematically evaluating 9 factors across 10 MOI methods and 10 TCGA cancer types.
MOSD introduced two key design principles: a composite evaluation metric (cluster-score) combining three complementary clustering metrics with equal weights, and a single-factor isolation design that varies only the factor under evaluation while controlling all others.
Weber et al.~\cite{weber2019benchmarking} established that rigorous benchmarking requires neutral evaluation, multiple metrics, controlled experimental design, and reproducibility; MOSD operationalized these principles for MOI, and these same design principles are transferred to MutBench for viral hotspot detection, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:mosd_summary}.

\section{Viral Mutation Hotspot Detection}
\label{sec:bg_hotspot}

\subsection{Mutation Hotspot Detection Methods}
\label{subsec:bg_hotspot_methods}

Mutation hotspots are regions within a genome where mutations are concentrated in a non-uniform manner, likely reflecting functionally important sites under structural or immunological selective pressure.
Identifying hotspots in viral genomes is critical for predicting variant emergence, selecting vaccine targets, and developing therapeutics.
Existing hotspot detection approaches can be categorized into frequency-based, entropy-based, and density-based methods.

\textbf{Frequency-based approaches} calculate mutation frequency at the amino acid or nucleotide level and designate positions with high frequency as hotspots.
While intuitive and easy to implement, they have two fundamental limitations.
First, \textbf{lineage bias}: when a specific lineage is overrepresented in the database, its defining mutations receive disproportionately high frequency scores.
Mutations such as D614G, observed in nearly all modern SARS-CoV-2 sequences, reflect genuine fitness advantages, but the rate of near-fixation is amplified by founder effects during the early pandemic, making it difficult to distinguish the relative contributions of selection and genetic drift from frequency data alone.
Second, \textbf{neglect of rare variants}: mutations that occur at low frequency but exhibit diverse forms---potentially indicating immune evasion or viral adaptation---are systematically overlooked.

\textbf{Entropy-based approaches} use Shannon entropy to measure nucleotide diversity at a given position.
Mullick et al.~\cite{mullick2021entropy} combined Shannon entropy with K-means clustering to detect hotspots in the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein.
However, conventional nucleotide entropy reflects the distribution of all nucleotides regardless of whether mutation has occurred, failing to capture effective diversity conditioned on the occurrence of mutation.
Furthermore, K-means clustering requires the number of clusters to be specified in advance and does not account for density structure.

\textbf{Cancer genomics methods.}
In cancer genomics, various methods have been developed to detect somatic mutation hotspots.
Cancer genomics tools such as MutSigCV~\cite{lawrence2013mutsigcv}, OncodriveCLUST~\cite{tamborero2013oncodriveclust}, HotMAPS~\cite{tokheim2016hotmaps}, and MutSpot~\cite{munoz2020mutspot} target somatic mutations in tumor genomes, which are more than 1,000 times larger than viral genomes and involve fundamentally different mutation dynamics and selective pressures, making direct transfer infeasible.

\subsection{Density-Based Clustering}
\label{subsec:bg_density_clustering}

DBSCAN~\cite{ester1996dbscan} is a representative density-based clustering algorithm that defines high-density regions as clusters and classifies low-density regions as noise.
It uses two key parameters---epsilon (search radius) and MinPts (minimum number of points)---to define density conditions.
However, DBSCAN uses a \textbf{fixed global epsilon}, making it unable to form appropriate clusters in data with substantial density variation.
This problem manifests in extreme form in viral genomes:
direct application to the SARS-CoV-2 genome with default parameters results in 99.76\% of the data being merged into a single massive cluster; even with manually tuned parameters ($\varepsilon = 0.15$, MinPts $= 5$), only 4 undifferentiated clusters are obtained, rendering biologically meaningful hotspot distinction impossible~\cite{youn2025mutclust}.

HDBSCAN~\cite{campello2013hdbscan} addresses the fundamental limitation of DBSCAN's fixed epsilon.
It hierarchically explores DBSCAN results across various epsilon values to adaptively extract clusters in response to density variation.
It operates with a single parameter, min\_cluster\_size, without requiring explicit specification of epsilon.
It can detect clusters of arbitrary shape, automatically identifies noise points, and provides probabilistic interpretation of cluster membership.

OPTICS~\cite{ankerst1999optics} generates a reachability plot revealing clustering structure at all density levels but requires post-processing for cluster extraction.

An important limitation of frequency-based mutation analysis is \textbf{phylogenetic non-independence}: mutations observed in closely related sequences are not independent events~\cite{felsenstein1985phylogenies}.
Phylogeny-aware methods such as TreeTime~\cite{sagulenko2018treetime}, BEAST~\cite{suchard2018beast}, and HyPhy~\cite{kosakovsky2020hyphy} can address this but require high-quality phylogenetic trees and have limited reliability in densely sampled datasets~\cite{turakhia2020ultrafast}.

\textbf{Deep Mutational Scanning (DMS)} is an experimental technique that systematically measures the functional effects of all possible single amino acid substitutions within a protein~\cite{fowler2014dms}.
For SARS-CoV-2, Starr et al.~\cite{starr2020dms} measured ACE2 binding affinity and expression in the receptor-binding domain (RBD), and Dadonaite et al.~\cite{dadonaite2023dms} measured pseudovirus cell entry fitness across the full-length Spike protein.
Bloom and Neher~\cite{bloom2023fitness} provided an integrated analysis of DMS fitness effects across all SARS-CoV-2 proteins.
DMS data provides a natural ground truth for hotspot benchmarking: positions where mutations exhibit large fitness effects correspond to biologically functional sites that detection methods should identify.


The absence of benchmark studies for hotspot detection methods in viral genomics stands in contrast to cancer genomics.
In cancer genomics, the comprehensive driver gene analysis by Bailey et al.~\cite{bailey2018comprehensive} serves as a standard benchmark, and numerous detection methods have been systematically compared against the same datasets and ground truth.
No such benchmark exists for viral genomes, and methods including MutClust rely solely on self-referential statistical tests.
This gap is the fundamental motivation for MutBench (Chapter~\ref{ch:mutbench}), the core study of this dissertation.

\section{MOSD: Multi-Omics Study Design Framework}
\label{sec:mosd_summary}

\textit{This section is a summary of the paper published in BMC Genomics (2025) 26:769~\cite{han2025mosd}.}


MOSD (Multi-Omics Study Design)~\cite{han2025mosd} is the first comprehensive benchmark framework that systematically evaluates the influence of 9 factors on 10 MOI methods across 10 TCGA cancer types.
Its three core contributions are:
(1)~a \textbf{composite cluster-score} that combines three complementary clustering metrics (ARI, NMI, F-measure) with equal weights to avoid single-metric bias;
(2)~a \textbf{single-factor isolation design} in which only the factor under evaluation is varied while all others are held constant;
and (3)~the central finding that \textbf{study design matters more than algorithm selection}---no single method dominated across all conditions, confirming that method selection is inherently context-dependent.

For this dissertation, MOSD's primary contribution is the \textbf{composite metric design principle}: the cluster-score's equal-weighted combination of complementary metrics is directly transferred to MutBench's hotspot-score $= (\text{recall} + \text{precision} + \text{stability}) / 3$.
MOSD's single-factor isolation design similarly informs the parameter sensitivity analysis in MutBench.
The framework-level principle---that evaluation factors must be systematically controlled and assessed through multiple metrics---directly shapes the design of MutBench.

\section{MutClust: Adaptive Density-Based Hotspot Detection}
\label{sec:mutclust_summary}

\textit{This section is a summary of the paper published in BioData Mining (2025) 18:61~\cite{youn2025mutclust}.}

\subsection{H-score and Adaptive DBSCAN}
\label{subsec:mutclust_hscore}

MutClust is the first density-based algorithm designed for detecting mutation hotspots in viral genomes.
Its core innovations are a novel mutation importance metric called the H-score and an adaptive DBSCAN framework built upon it.

The \textbf{H-score} combines mutation frequency and mutation entropy (diversity conditioned on mutation occurrence) through a logarithmic transformation of their product, thereby simultaneously capturing two complementary signals while suppressing two classes of false positives: lineage-defining mutations with high frequency but low diversity, and noise positions with low frequency but spuriously high diversity.

Built upon the H-score, \textbf{Adaptive DBSCAN} replaces DBSCAN's fixed global epsilon with a neighborhood radius proportional to each position's H-score, assigning wider search radii to biologically significant regions and narrower radii elsewhere.
The algorithm first identifies Core Candidate Mutations (CCMs) that satisfy threshold conditions on individual H-score, neighborhood mean, neighborhood sum, and minimum point count, then expands clusters bidirectionally from each CCM with a diminishing radius.
The key parameters ($\gamma=10$, $d=3$, MinPts$=5$) were empirically determined from 224,318 SARS-CoV-2 sequences, and the overall time complexity is $O(N + M \log M)$ where $N$ is genome length and $M$ is the number of mutation positions.

\subsection{SARS-CoV-2 Hotspot Detection Results}
\label{subsec:mutclust_results_summary}

Analysis of 224,318 GISAID SARS-CoV-2 sequences aligned to the Wuhan-Hu-1 reference genome (NC\_045512.2, 29,903 bp) revealed that MutClust detected \textbf{477} mutation hotspots, compared to only 4 clusters obtained by DBSCAN with tuned parameters---a qualitatively different level of resolution.
Of 10 known functional mutations examined for validation, 9 were contained within hotspots, including D614G, E484K, N501Y, and L452R, and the highest hotspot density was observed in the Spike protein region.
Validation on influenza confirmed partial generalizability but highlighted the need for parameter readjustment across different genome sizes. For H3N2 in particular, Smith et al.~\cite{smith2004antigenic} demonstrated through antigenic cartography that a small number of substitutions in the HA protein drive major antigenic change, underscoring the importance of accurate detection of such key positions.

\subsection{Limitations and Motivation for MutBench Development}
\label{subsec:mutclust_limitations}

Despite the achievements of MutClust, three fundamental limitations serve as the direct motivation for MutBench development.

\textbf{First, implementation-level issues.}
A bug in the diminishing formula of the cluster expansion function causes cumulative rather than linear radius decay, leading to premature termination of cluster expansion and, in certain edge cases, reversal of the decay direction.

\textbf{Second, hardcoded parameters.}
$\gamma=10$, $d=3$, and the CCM thresholds (0.03, 0.01, 0.05) are empirically determined values fitted to 224,318 SARS-CoV-2 sequences.
These values may become inappropriate when the number of sequences or genome size differs, yet the sensitivity of detection results to parameter variation has never been systematically analyzed.
Although applying the same parameters was successful in influenza validation, influenza genomes are approximately one-tenth the size of SARS-CoV-2 ($\sim$2,300 bp/segment vs. $\sim$30,000 bp), and the necessity of parameter readjustment for best performance remains unresolved.

\textbf{Third, the absence of systematic benchmarking.}
The hotspot detection performance of MutClust relies solely on qualitative comparison with known functional mutations, and quantitative evaluation (precision, recall, stability) based on independent ground truth has not been conducted.
In cancer genomics, systematic comparison studies of hotspot detection methods have been performed, such as the comprehensive driver gene analysis by Bailey et al.~\cite{bailey2018comprehensive}, but no such benchmark exists for viral genomes.
Viral hotspot detection methods, including MutClust, rely solely on self-referential statistical tests (bootstrap), and the absence of standardized evaluation metrics makes fair comparison of results across different methods impossible.

Additionally, since H-scores are computed at the nucleotide level, they do not distinguish between synonymous and non-synonymous mutations, necessitating integration with selective pressure indicators such as the dN/dS ratio.
Phylogenetic non-independence---the possibility that mutation frequency is the result of a single founder effect rather than independent positive selection---is also not corrected for.

\vspace{1em}

The core of these limitations is that MutClust proposed a new detection method but failed to simultaneously provide a framework for quantitatively evaluating that method's performance.
This corresponds to the absence of the benchmark principles reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:bg_moi}---neutral evaluation, multiple metrics, and controlled experimental design.
