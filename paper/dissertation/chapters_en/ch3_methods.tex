% Chapter 3: Materials and Method
\chapter{Materials and Method}
\label{ch:mutbench}

This chapter presents the methodology of MutBench, a benchmark framework for the systematic evaluation of viral mutation hotspot detection methods.
The overall pipeline processes multiple sequence alignment (MSA) data from 9 RNA viruses through 9 scoring formulas and 41 detection methods, evaluating results against a 3-layer ground truth system using the hotspot-score composite metric and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC).
The evaluation follows a two-stage design: Stage~1 conducts in-depth analysis on SARS-CoV-2 to validate the framework components, and Stage~2 extends the evaluation to all 9 pathogens for large-scale cross-pathogen comparison.
Section~\ref{sec:mutbench_materials} describes the materials (target pathogens, ground truth framework, and DMS data), Sections~\ref{sec:mutbench_methods}--\ref{sec:eval_stat_design} present the methods: Section~\ref{sec:mutbench_methods} covers the benchmark framework design including MutClust variants and the hotspot-score metric, Section~\ref{sec:scoring_detection} describes the scoring formulas and detection method families, and Section~\ref{sec:eval_stat_design} details the evaluation metrics and statistical analysis design.
\label{sec:9pathogen_benchmark}

\subsection{Target Pathogens and Data}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_data}

Nine pathogens are targeted: SARS-CoV-2, Influenza A H3N2 (H3N2), Norovirus, HIV-1, Dengue, RSV (Respiratory Syncytial Virus), Influenza B, MERS-CoV (MERS), and HCV (Hepatitis C Virus).
FASTA sequences for each pathogen were collected from NCBI GenBank by querying each target protein name (e.g., ``SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein'') with filters for complete coding sequences, excluding partial sequences and laboratory-derived constructs. No date range restriction was applied; all available sequences as of December 2024 were downloaded. Sequences with $>$5\% ambiguous bases (N) were removed, and identical sequences were deduplicated (the ``Unique seq.'' column in Table~\ref{tab:pathogen_data} reflects post-deduplication counts). Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was performed for each surface protein (SARS-CoV-2: Spike, H3N2: HA, HIV-1: Env gp120, etc.) using MAFFT v7.505~\cite{katoh2013mafft} with the \texttt{--auto} mode, followed by computation of per-position mutation frequency and entropy.
The \texttt{--auto} mode automatically selects among FFT-NS-2, L-INS-i, and G-INS-i based on the number and length of input sequences~\cite{katoh2013mafft}.
At the sequence scales used in this study (1,311--5,325 sequences, Table~\ref{tab:pathogen_data}), FFT-NS-2 is typically selected, but the alignment algorithm may differ across pathogens, and the effect of this difference on alignment quality---and consequently on per-position mutation frequency computation---was not controlled in this study.
When alignment lengths differed across sequences, all sequences were truncated to the minimum sequence length within the MSA to ensure consistency in per-position comparisons.
Ground truth positions exceeding the alignment length are thereby excluded from evaluation (the position counts in Table~\ref{tab:gt_positions} are effective values after this truncation).

\label{subsec:3layer_gt}

\textbf{Design rationale: three orthogonal dimensions.}
The three layers are deliberately designed to capture different dimensions of mutational importance.
Layer~A asks ``does this position mutate repeatedly and independently?'' (an evolutionary \textit{pattern} question), without requiring knowledge of why the mutation is advantageous---whether it confers immune evasion, increased transmissibility, or drug resistance.
Layer~B asks ``is this position structurally constrained?'' (a \textit{structural} question), identifying positions where any mutation destroys protein function.
Layer~C asks ``what is the measurable functional impact of mutations at this position?'' (a \textit{functional} question), using laboratory DMS experiments that directly quantify how each amino acid substitution affects viral fitness (e.g., cell entry efficiency, receptor binding affinity).
This separation means each layer provides an independent, non-redundant perspective: Layer~A captures evolutionary signals that DMS cannot (multi-lineage emergence patterns require years of surveillance data), while Layer~C captures functional effects that convergent evolution analysis cannot (a position may have strong fitness effects but not yet show convergent evolution if the virus has not encountered sufficient selection pressure).

\subsubsection{Layer A: Adaptive Positive Selection}

Convergent evolution positions---positions where mutations recurrently arose in multiple independent lineages \textit{within the same viral species}---are classified as hotspots under positive selection.
For example, the E484K mutation in SARS-CoV-2 emerged independently in the Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Omicron lineages, each of which descended from different ancestral branches of the SARS-CoV-2 phylogenetic tree; this repeated independent emergence at the same position provides strong evidence of positive selection at that site.
Importantly, Layer~A does not specify the biological mechanism driving the selection---the same criterion applies whether the mutation enables immune evasion, enhances receptor binding, increases transmissibility, or confers drug resistance.
The criterion is purely phylogenetic: recurrent independent emergence in $\geq$3 lineages.
These positions are used as \textbf{True Positives}, evaluating whether detection methods correctly identify them as hotspots.

\textbf{Identification criteria.}
For each pathogen, Layer~A positions were compiled from published studies that identified convergent evolution sites (Table~\ref{tab:gt_positions}, rightmost column).
The inclusion criterion was that the position must exhibit recurrent nonsynonymous mutations arising independently in $\geq$3 phylogenetically distinct lineages, as documented in the cited studies.
For example, SARS-CoV-2 Layer~A positions (e.g., E484, N501, L452, K417) were identified by Carabelli et al.~\cite{carabelli2023convergent} through phylogenetic analysis showing independent emergence across Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron lineages.
For HCV, the hypervariable regions HVR1 and HVR2 were used, as these regions exhibit diversifying selection across independent infections.
No computational tool was applied to identify new convergent evolution positions; all positions were taken directly from the cited literature.

\subsubsection{Layer B: Constrained Purifying Selection}

Structurally and functionally essential sites under purifying selection are identified as true negatives.
These are positions where mutations are actively \textit{removed} from the viral population because they destroy protein folding, disrupt membrane fusion, or abolish receptor binding---any mutation here is lethal or severely deleterious to the virus.
Unlike Layer~A (which identifies positions under \textit{positive} selection, where change is advantageous), Layer~B identifies positions under \textit{purifying} selection (where change is harmful).
These positions exhibit low mutation rates due to loss-of-function consequences and are used as \textbf{True Negatives}.
When detection methods incorrectly identify these positions as hotspots, they are counted as false positives, and the constrained false positive rate (constrained FPR) is computed.

\textbf{Identification criteria.}
Layer~B positions were identified based on two types of evidence from the cited literature: (1)~structural essentiality---positions within domains whose disruption abolishes protein function (e.g., fusion peptide, HR1/HR2 heptad repeats, structural disulfide-bonding cysteine residues), as determined by published mutagenesis or structural studies; and (2)~evolutionary conservation---positions exhibiting dN/dS $< 0.5$ across diverse viral isolates, indicating strong purifying selection against amino acid changes.
For SARS-CoV-2, the 154 Layer~B positions include the HR1 (AA 912--984) and HR2 (AA 1,163--1,213) heptad repeats and structural cysteine residues, identified from the cryo-EM structure~\cite{wrapp2020cryo} and conservation analysis.
As with Layer~A, no de novo computational identification was performed; all positions were curated from published sources.

\subsubsection{Layer C: DMS Experimental Validation}

For four pathogens with available Deep Mutational Scanning (DMS) experimental data---SARS-CoV-2~\cite{dadonaite2023dms}, HIV-1, H3N2, and RSV---experimentally measured fitness effects are used as ground truth.
Unlike Layer~A (which infers importance from evolutionary patterns) and Layer~B (which infers importance from structural conservation), Layer~C provides direct experimental measurements: in DMS experiments, every possible single amino acid substitution at every position is physically constructed in the laboratory, and its effect on a quantifiable phenotype (e.g., cell entry efficiency for SARS-CoV-2, replicative fitness for H3N2) is measured.
Positions in the top 20th percentile of mean absolute fitness effect from DMS data are classified as functionally important positions, and the DMS F1 score is computed against detection results.
This layer is particularly valuable because it captures functional importance that may not yet be visible in evolutionary data (a position may have strong fitness effects but insufficient time for convergent evolution to manifest).

\textbf{Layer A/B overlap handling.}
In RSV (positions 148, 152) and Influenza B (positions 141, 194), overlapping positions exist between Layer A and Layer B.
This occurs because these residues are simultaneously under immune evasion pressure (positive selection) and structural constraints (purifying selection), representing dual-selection sites.
In MCC evaluation, these positions are prioritized as Layer A (positive) and excluded from the Layer B (negative) set.
Therefore, the effective Layer B for RSV is 16 (18 $-$ 2), and for Influenza B is 8 (10 $-$ 2) positions.

\subsection{DMS-based Benchmark}
\label{subsec:dms_ground_truth}

The DMS fitness data are derived from the full-length Spike protein characterization experiment by Dadonaite et al.~\cite{dadonaite2023dms}.
Pseudovirus cell entry fitness measurements are used, as this is the most directly relevant phenotype capturing the overall functional impact of mutations on viral entry.
Amino acid positions in the top 20th percentile of mean absolute fitness effect are selected as functional sites and mapped to nucleotide coordinates through codon-aware coordinate transformation:
\begin{equation}
\text{nuc\_positions} = \{g_{\text{start}} + (p_{\text{aa}} - 1) \times 3 + k : k \in \{0, 1, 2\}\}
\label{eq:coordinate_transform}
\end{equation}
where $g_{\text{start}} = 21{,}563$ is the start position of the Spike gene in the Wuhan-Hu-1 reference genome.

This provides an experimental ground truth independent of the detection methods under evaluation, thereby avoiding the circularity of using bootstrap p-values for validation.

\subsubsection{Component 1: Ground Truth Generation}

Ground truth is generated from two sources.

\textbf{Synthetic data (controlled signal recovery test).}
The synthetic benchmark was designed as a controlled signal recovery test that evaluates how accurately each detection method recovers artificially inserted signals at known positions.
Synthetic H-scores are generated mimicking SARS-CoV-2 patterns, with hotspot regions inserted at known functional sites.
High H-scores (0.8--1.2) are assigned to Core Candidate Mutation (CCM) seed positions (AA 484, 501, 417, 452---the four Spike positions most frequently mutated in major SARS-CoV-2 variants), moderate scores (0.05--0.3) to surrounding functional regions, and low noise (0.0--0.02) to background positions.
Scores within each category are drawn from uniform distributions: $\mathcal{U}(0.8, 1.2)$ for seed positions, $\mathcal{U}(0.05, 0.3)$ for surrounding functional regions (defined as positions within 50 positions of each seed), and $\mathcal{U}(0.0, 0.02)$ for background. A single synthetic genome of 29,903 positions is generated per trial.
Since the ground truth of synthetic data is known by design, this test evaluates each method's signal recovery capability but does not directly measure actual biological hotspot detection performance.
Therefore, DMS-based validation (Section~\ref{subsec:dms_ground_truth}) and convergent evolution evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:multi_gt}) provide independent biological validation, confirming whether the conclusions from the synthetic benchmark hold on real data.

\textbf{DMS-based functional sites}: From cell entry fitness measurements in the full-length Spike protein DMS experiment~\cite{dadonaite2023dms}, amino acid positions in the top 20th percentile of mean absolute fitness effect are classified as functional sites.

The annotated functional regions used as ground truth are presented in Table~\ref{tab:ground_truth}, with region boundaries based on the cryo-EM structure of the prefusion Spike trimer~\cite{wrapp2020cryo} and the NTD antigenic supersite mapping by McCallum et al.~\cite{mccallum2021ntd}.

\subsubsection{Component 3: Evaluation Metrics}

Position-level precision, recall, F1, enrichment ratio, subsampling stability, and the hotspot-score newly defined in this study are used as evaluation metrics.

\subsection{Five MutClust Variants}
\label{subsec:variants}

\subsubsection{H-score Computation}
\label{subsec:mutbench_hscore}

MutClust computes an H-score for each genomic position:
\begin{equation}
H_i = \log_2(P_i \times E_i \times 100 + 1)
\label{eq:mutbench_hscore}
\end{equation}
where $P_i$ is the sum of mutation proportions at position $i$ and $E_i$ is the Shannon entropy of mutation types.
The constant 100 is inherited from the original MutClust formulation~\cite{youn2025mutclust}; its role is to scale the product $P_i \times E_i$ (which ranges from 0 to approximately 0.01 for typical mutation frequencies) into a range where the $\log_2$ transform produces meaningful discrimination between positions.
Since all methods in the benchmark use the same H-score formula, the constant affects absolute score values but not the relative ranking of positions, which is what detection methods operate on.
Shannon entropy is defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
E_i = -\sum_{j=1}^{k} p_{ij} \log_2 p_{ij}
\label{eq:shannon_entropy}
\end{equation}
where $p_{ij}$ is the relative frequency of mutation type $j$ at position $i$ and $k$ is the number of observed mutation types.
The H-score combines mutation frequency (how often a position mutates) and mutation diversity (how many substitution types occur).

Positions satisfying the CCM (Core Candidate Mutation) criteria---H-score above a threshold, sufficient neighbor density, and minimum point count---are selected as seeds.
The expand\_cluster function extends each seed bidirectionally using dependency-based decay:
\begin{equation}
D_\epsilon(t) = D_\epsilon^{\text{CCM}} - \frac{t}{d}
\label{eq:expand_cluster}
\end{equation}
where $D_\epsilon^{\text{CCM}} = \lceil \gamma \times H_{\text{seed}} \rceil$ is the initial dependency range of the seed, $t$ is the distance from the seed, and $d$ is the decay factor.

\subsubsection{Bug Fix (MutClust-Fixed)}
\label{subsec:improvements}

The expand\_cluster function of MutClust-Original contained a bug in which the dependency range was subtracted cumulatively at each step rather than computed directly from the seed, causing accelerated range contraction and a loss of 71--95\% of the theoretical detection range.
MutClust-Fixed corrects this by computing the dependency range directly from the seed ($D_\epsilon(t) = D_\epsilon^{\text{CCM}} - t/d$), which slightly increased detected positions in the synthetic benchmark (641 vs. 637) and yielded clusters extending to their theoretically predicted boundaries.

\subsubsection{dN/dS Selection Filter (MutClust-dNdS)}

MutClust-dNdS adds post-detection filtering based on the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous mutations (dN/dS).

\textbf{dN/dS computation method.}
The dN/dS ratio is computed per cluster using the Nei--Gojobori simple counting method~\cite{nei1986simple}.
Only clusters under positive selection (dN/dS $> 1.0$) are retained; clusters under neutral or purifying selection are removed.
In short clusters (fewer than 10 codons) where synonymous substitutions may be zero, dN/dS is undefined and the cluster is conservatively excluded.

\textbf{Nucleotide vs. amino acid level.}
The evaluation in this study is performed at the nucleotide (nt) level.
The Spike gene (nt 21,563--25,384) comprises 3,822 nucleotides (1,273 amino acids + stop codon), and H-scores are computed per nt position.
Since ground truth functional regions are defined at the amino acid level (e.g., RBD = AA 319--541), each AA position is expanded to its corresponding 3 codon positions to construct nt-level ground truth for evaluation.
The nt-level evaluation was chosen for two reasons: (1) the H-score independently captures mutation frequency even at the third codon position (wobble position), and (2) a consistent coordinate system is maintained in anticipation of future extension to non-coding regions (5$'$/3$'$-UTR).

\textbf{Insertion/deletion (indel) handling.}
The H-score computation in MutClust targets only single nucleotide substitutions; insertions and deletions are currently not handled.
This means that biologically important indels such as ins214EPE (Alpha/BA.1), $\Delta$69--70 (Alpha), and $\Delta$Y144 (Alpha) are not reflected in the H-score.
Future work should integrate indel frequencies as a separate dimension or assign weights to positions where indels occur.

Purifying selection (dN/dS $< 1$) also indicates biologically important functionally constrained regions---for example, the fusion machinery (HR1/HR2) and essential structural domains.
Therefore, the dN/dS $> 1.0$ filter sacrifices recall of these conserved functional regions in exchange for improved precision in identifying sites under active positive selection, such as immune evasion and receptor adaptation.

\textbf{Jukes-Cantor correction validation.}
Jukes-Cantor correction produced negligible changes ($\rho = 0.996$); the uncorrected method was retained.

\subsubsection{HDBSCAN Hybrid (MutClust-Hybrid)}

MutClust-Hybrid combines MutClust's domain-specific CCM seed detection with HDBSCAN's~\cite{campello2013hdbscan} automatic cluster boundary determination:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Seed identification}: MutClust's \texttt{is\_ccm()} function is used to preserve domain knowledge regarding mutation hotspot characteristics.
\item \textbf{Candidate collection}: All positions with non-zero H-scores within the dependency range of each CCM seed are collected.
\item \textbf{HDBSCAN clustering}: Candidate positions are clustered by HDBSCAN in a two-dimensional feature space consisting of (i) genomic position min-max normalized to the $[0, H_{\max}]$ range and (ii) H-score magnitude. This normalization assigns equal weight to spatial proximity and score similarity.
\item \textbf{dN/dS filtering}: The same positive selection signal filter as MutClust-dNdS is applied.
\end{enumerate}

This hybrid approach retains MutClust's biological prior knowledge (CCM criteria) while leveraging HDBSCAN's data-driven boundary detection.

\textbf{Computational complexity.}
The overall complexity of MutClust-Hybrid decomposes into three stages:
(1)~H-score computation: $O(NM)$ for $N$ sequences and $M$ positions;
(2)~CCM seed identification and candidate collection: linear scan after H-score sorting $O(M \log M)$;
(3)~HDBSCAN clustering: $O(k^2)$ for $k$ candidate positions around CCM seeds (minimum spanning tree construction).
In practice, $k \ll M$ (in the synthetic benchmark, $k \approx 800$, $M = 3{,}822$), so the overall complexity is dominated by $O(NM + M \log M)$, with H-score computation as the bottleneck.
For $N = 10{,}000$ sequences and $M = 3{,}822$ positions, the total pipeline execution time is approximately 2 seconds (single core), making it practical even for real-time surveillance settings.

\subsubsection{Pure HDBSCAN (HDBSCAN-Auto)}

HDBSCAN-Auto completely bypasses MutClust and applies HDBSCAN directly to positions above an adaptive threshold (median of non-zero H-scores).
It uses the same two-dimensional feature space (min-max normalized position, H-score) and requires no manually specified domain parameters, enabling application to any viral genome without prior tuning.
HDBSCAN-Auto uses min\_cluster\_size $= 5$ and min\_samples $= 3$ as default parameters.

\subsection{Combined Evaluation Metric (Hotspot-score)}
\label{subsec:hotspot_score}

The hotspot-score, one of the key methodological features of this study, is a composite evaluation metric that integrates three complementary perspectives:

\begin{equation}
\text{hotspot-score} = \frac{\text{recall} + \text{precision} + \text{stability}}{3}
\label{eq:hotspot_score}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Recall}: The proportion of known functional positions captured within detected hotspot regions. It evaluates biological relevance---does the method find what is important?
\item \textbf{Precision}: The proportion of detected positions that overlap with ground truth. It evaluates specificity---does the method avoid false positives?
\item \textbf{Stability}: The mean Jaccard similarity between the full-data detection and 100 subsampling runs (80\% position retention, position-level zeroing). It evaluates reproducibility---does the method produce consistent results under data perturbation?
\end{itemize}

The three components receive equal weights. In viral surveillance applications, a method with high accuracy but instability under data perturbation is less practical than a method with moderate accuracy and high reproducibility; including stability as an equal component reflects this requirement.

\noindent The reason for separating recall and precision and combining them with stability, rather than using the F1 score, is that F1 collapses recall and precision into a single value, thereby obscuring their interaction with stability.
For example, HDBSCAN-Auto has a low F1 (0.146) but high recall (0.944) and stability (0.788).
Under an F1 + stability structure, this method would be undervalued, whereas under the (recall + precision + stability)/3 structure, the high recall and stability are appropriately reflected.

\section{Scoring and Detection Methods}
\label{sec:scoring_detection}

\subsection{Scoring Formulas}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_scoring}

Nine scoring formulas are defined to assign scores to each genomic position.
Here, $P_i$ denotes the mutation frequency at position $i$, $E_i$ the Shannon entropy, and $\text{rare}_i$ the rare variant frequency (the proportion of variants with frequency below 5\% among all variants).

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{E*rare}: $S_i = E_i \times \text{rare}_i$. The product of entropy and rare variant frequency, emphasizing positions with high diversity and abundant rare variants.
\item \textbf{P*E*rare}: $S_i = P_i \times E_i \times \text{rare}_i$. Combines mutation frequency, entropy, and rare variants.
\item \textbf{minority\_E}: $S_i = f_{2\text{nd},i} \times E_i$. The product of the second most frequent allele frequency ($f_{2\text{nd}}$) and entropy.
\item \textbf{P*minority\_E}: $S_i = P_i \times f_{2\text{nd},i} \times E_i$.
\item \textbf{P*E}: $S_i = P_i \times E_i$. The simple product of frequency and entropy.
\item \textbf{H-score}: $S_i = \log_2(P_i \times E_i \times 100 + 1)$. The original MutClust formula (Equation~\ref{eq:mutbench_hscore}).
\item \textbf{E\_only}: $S_i = E_i$. Uses Shannon entropy alone.
\item \textbf{P*E\textsuperscript{2}}: $S_i = P_i \times E_i^2$. Applies quadratic weighting to entropy.
\item \textbf{rank(P*E)}: $S_i = \text{rank}(P_i \times E_i) / N$. A nonparametric score normalizing the rank of P*E.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scoring Variants for Multi-Source Ground Truth}
\label{subsec:alternative_scoring}

As H-score saturation was identified in the temporal validation (Section~\ref{sec:temporal}), the following alternative scoring methods were designed to perform complementary evaluation against various ground truth definitions.
These methods are not hotspot ``detection'' methods but rather ``scoring'' approaches that assign a score to each genomic position; top positions are selected via thresholding and compared against multiple ground truths.

\textbf{Original H-score}: Uses MutClust's original H-score formula (Equation~\ref{eq:mutbench_hscore}) as-is.

\textbf{Saturated H-score}: H-score computed through contemporary consensus sequence alignment on 2024--2025 data, reflecting within-population variation rather than comparison against the reference sequence (Wuhan-Hu-1).

\textbf{CH-score (Consensus-based H-score)}: An H-score variant that removes reference sequence dependency by computing mutation frequency relative to the current consensus sequence:
\begin{equation}
\text{CH}_i = \log_2(P_i^{\text{consensus}} \times E_i \times 100 + 1)
\end{equation}
where $P_i^{\text{consensus}}$ is the non-consensus amino acid frequency relative to the consensus sequence.

\textbf{E-score (Entropy-only score)}: Uses Shannon entropy alone, removing the frequency term ($P_i$) and scoring positions solely by diversity:
\begin{equation}
\text{E-score}_i = -\sum_{a} f_{a,i} \log_2 f_{a,i}
\end{equation}

\textbf{TC-freq (Temporal Contrast frequency)}: Measures the rate of change in mutation frequency between two time periods:
\begin{equation}
\text{TC-freq}_i = \frac{|\text{freq}_{t_2}(i) - \text{freq}_{t_1}(i)|}{\text{freq}_{t_1}(i) + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
where $\text{freq}_{t}(i)$ is the non-reference amino acid frequency at position $i$ in time period $t$, and $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ is a constant to prevent division by zero.

\textbf{TC-freq-v2}: A variant of TC-freq with added absolute frequency weighting, defined as $\text{TC-freq-v2}_i = \text{TC-freq}_i \times \text{freq}_{t_2}(i)$.

\textbf{TC-freq-v3}: A variant using quarterly time windows, taking the maximum frequency change across each quarter rather than the full annual comparison.

\textbf{dN/dS-weighted CH}: A variant that applies position-level dN/dS ratios as weights to the CH-score.

For each scoring method, thresholds at the top 5\%, 10\%, 20\%, and median are applied to generate sets of ``detected'' positions, and F1, enrichment ratio, and Cohen's $d$ effect size are computed for each ground truth.

\noindent The 15 families are grouped into five categories below; each family is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:detection_families}.

\textbf{Threshold-based} (FreqThresh, AdaptiveKnee, AdaptWin).
These methods select hotspots by applying a score cutoff: FreqThresh uses a fixed percentile threshold (top $k$\%), AdaptiveKnee automatically determines the threshold via knee-point detection on the sorted score curve, and AdaptWin extends seed positions above a percentile threshold bidirectionally to adjacent high-scoring positions.
Frequency-based thresholding is widely used in viral genomic surveillance; for example, Mercatelli and Giorgi~\cite{mercatelli2020geographic} applied mutation frequency thresholds to identify recurrent SARS-CoV-2 mutations across geographic regions.

\textbf{Signal processing} (Wavelet, Spectral, CUSUM).
Transform-based methods that convert the score profile into an alternative representation to identify anomalous regions: Wavelet applies continuous wavelet transform with a Mexican hat kernel across multiple scales, Spectral uses FFT-based high-pass filtering to isolate local deviations, and CUSUM applies cumulative sum control charts to detect change points in the $z$-normalized profile.
Wavelet-based approaches have been applied to genomic sequence analysis, including Anastassiou~\cite{anastassiou2001genomic} who demonstrated wavelet transforms for detecting periodicities in DNA sequences, and Arneodo et al.~\cite{arneodo2011wavelet} who used multi-scale wavelet analysis to characterize long-range correlations in genomic sequences.

\textbf{Density and clustering} (KDE, ScoreDBSCAN).
KDE applies Gaussian kernel density estimation to the score-weighted position profile and thresholds by percentile. ScoreDBSCAN maps positions to a 2D (coordinate, score) space and applies DBSCAN clustering with rescaled axes.
Density-based clustering was originally proposed by Ester et al.~\cite{ester1996dbscan} and has been adapted for mutation hotspot detection in cancer genomics; notably, Tokheim et al.~\cite{tokheim2016hotmaps} applied density-based clustering to 3D protein structures for exome-scale hotspot discovery.

\textbf{Statistical and model-based} (SlidingTest, LocalContrast, GradPeak, Bayes, SWAN).
These methods apply statistical tests to identify positions or windows significantly deviating from the background: SlidingTest and SWAN use sliding-window $z$-tests against the global mean, LocalContrast computes local $z$-scores, GradPeak detects prominence-filtered peaks on smoothed profiles, and Bayes computes posterior hotspot probabilities from a prior and normalized scores.
Sliding window approaches are a standard technique in population genetics and genomic analysis; Tajima~\cite{tajima1989statistical} introduced sliding window computation of the D statistic for detecting departures from neutrality, and similar windowed scanning strategies have been widely adopted for identifying regions under selection in viral genomes.

\textbf{Ensemble and legacy} (Ensemble, MutClust-Orig).
Ensemble combines four simple sub-detectors via majority voting. MutClust-Orig applies the original MutClust logic (CCM seed + adaptive DBSCAN) to MSA-based score profiles.
Ensemble strategies that aggregate multiple detection methods have been shown to improve robustness in computational genomics benchmarks~\cite{bailey2018comprehensive}; the rationale is that combining diverse algorithmic perspectives mitigates individual method biases.

\section{Evaluation and Statistical Design}
\label{sec:eval_stat_design}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_metrics}

The following evaluation metrics are used in the 9-pathogen benchmark.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{MCC (Matthews Correlation Coefficient)}: The primary evaluation metric (defined formally in Equation~\ref{eq:mcc} below).
The confusion matrix is constructed using only Layer A (positive selection positions) as positive labels: TP = detected positions belonging to Layer A, FP = detected positions not belonging to Layer A, FN = undetected positions belonging to Layer A, TN = undetected positions not belonging to Layer A.
Layer B (constrained regions) is not included in MCC computation and is evaluated separately via constrained FPR. The majority of genomic positions belonging to neither Layer A nor B are classified as FP or TN depending on detection status.
\item \textbf{F1}: The harmonic mean of precision and recall.
\item \textbf{Precision}: The proportion of true positives among detected positions.
\item \textbf{Recall}: The proportion of true positives detected among all true positives.
\item \textbf{Enrichment ratio}: The ratio of positive density within detected regions to overall positive density.
\item \textbf{Constrained FPR}: The false positive rate in Layer B constrained regions.
\item \textbf{DMS F1}: F1 score against Layer C DMS ground truth (computed only for 4 pathogens).
\end{itemize}

While Stage~1 uses hotspot-score (which includes stability) for in-depth single-pathogen analysis, Stage~2 adopts MCC as the primary metric because stability has not been validated for cross-pathogen comparability and MCC provides a balanced evaluation for the extreme class imbalance present in multi-pathogen evaluation.
This design decision is motivated by three considerations.
First, the stability component measures bootstrap reproducibility under position-level subsampling, but the subsampling behavior differs fundamentally across pathogens with different alignment lengths (340--1,330 AA) and MSA sizes (530--5,019 unique sequences), making cross-pathogen stability values non-comparable.
Second, MCC utilizes all four cells of the confusion matrix (TP, TN, FP, FN), providing a balanced evaluation under the extreme class imbalance inherent in hotspot detection (positive rates 1--16\% across the 9 pathogens).
Third, separating the evaluation metric between stages allows Stage~1 to assess detection \textit{reliability} (via stability) and Stage~2 to assess detection \textit{accuracy} (via MCC), each optimized for its analytical purpose.
MCC is formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{MCC} = \frac{TP \times TN - FP \times FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}
\label{eq:mcc}
\end{equation}
where $TP$, $TN$, $FP$, and $FN$ denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively.
MCC ranges from $-1$ (complete disagreement) through $0$ (random prediction) to $+1$ (perfect prediction), and unlike F1, it utilizes all four cells of the confusion matrix, making it robust to class imbalance.
When any factor in the denominator is zero (e.g., no positive predictions), MCC is conventionally defined as 0.
MCC was selected as the primary metric because in hotspot detection, where positive positions (convergent evolution sites) constitute a minority of the genome, it provides a more balanced evaluation than F1 for imbalanced classification problems. Chicco and Jurman~\cite{chicco2020mcc} systematically demonstrated that MCC provides more informative evaluation than F1 and accuracy in imbalanced binary classification.
The stability component used in Stage 1 is excluded from Stage 2 because it is an internal consistency metric independent of external ground truth, and cross-pathogen comparability of Bernoulli masking effects has not been validated given the wide variation in sequence length and MSA size across the 9 pathogens.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Design}
\label{subsec:9pathogen_statistics}

\subsubsection{Three-way ANOVA ($\omega^2$)}

A three-way ANOVA is performed with scoring formula (9 levels), detection family (15 levels), and pathogen (9 levels) as factors.
Bias-corrected $\omega^2$ (omega-squared) is used as the effect size measure, which avoids the upward bias of $\eta^2$ with sample size. Following Cohen's effect size benchmarks~\cite{cohen1988statistical}, $\omega^2$ values are interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large ($\geq 0.14$):
\begin{equation}
\omega^2 = \frac{SS_{\text{effect}} - df_{\text{effect}} \times MS_{\text{error}}}{SS_{\text{total}} + MS_{\text{error}}}
\label{eq:omega_squared}
\end{equation}
where $SS_{\text{effect}}$ is the sum of squares for the factor of interest, $df_{\text{effect}}$ its degrees of freedom, $MS_{\text{error}}$ the mean square of the residual error, and $SS_{\text{total}}$ the total sum of squares.
The $\omega^2$ values of main effects and two-way interactions are compared to identify the primary sources of performance variation.

\subsubsection{LOPO Cross-Validation (Leave-One-Pathogen-Out)}

The best-performing combination (scoring $\times$ detection) is selected from 8 of 9 pathogens, applied to the held-out pathogen, and generalization performance is evaluated.
Through 9 iterations, the concordance rate between training-best and test-best (oracle) combinations and the performance gap are computed.

\subsubsection{Friedman Test}

A nonparametric repeated-measures test using 9 pathogens as blocks, testing whether statistically significant performance differences exist among the top $k$ combinations.

\subsubsection{Pathogen-level BCa Bootstrap Confidence Intervals}

Bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals are computed for MCC values across 9 pathogens to quantify the performance variability of the best-performing combination across pathogens.

\subsubsection{Subsample Robustness Analysis}

Random subsamples at 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, and 100\% of MSA sequences are drawn, and the benchmark is repeated to verify MCC stability as a function of sample size.

\textbf{Weight Sensitivity Analysis.}
\label{subsec:weight_sensitivity_methods}
To verify the robustness of assigning equal weights ($1/3$) to the three components of the hotspot-score, a generalized weighted hotspot-score is defined:
\begin{equation}
\text{hotspot-score}_w = w_r \cdot \text{recall} + w_p \cdot \text{precision} + w_s \cdot \text{stability}
\label{eq:weighted_hotspot_score}
\end{equation}
where $w_r + w_p + w_s = 1$ and each weight lies in the range $[0.05, 0.95]$.
Rankings of the five methods are computed across a total of 171 weight combinations to analyze the proportion of weight space in which MutClust-Hybrid maintains first place (rank robustness).
Additionally, rankings are separately presented for seven application scenarios, including early warning (recall-emphasis), vaccine design (precision-emphasis), and monitoring (stability-emphasis).

\textbf{Statistical Validation Methods.}
\label{subsec:statistical_methods}
The following three methods are applied to assess the statistical robustness of the benchmark results.

\textit{Bootstrap confidence intervals}: Region-level bootstrap is performed using the seven functional regions as units.
In each iteration, seven regions are sampled with replacement to construct a bootstrap ground truth, and the detection results from the full data are re-evaluated against this bootstrap ground truth.
This design captures the uncertainty in ground truth definition---variation in evaluation depending on which functional regions are included---and avoids the effective size reduction bias that occurs with individual position resampling.

\textit{Permutation test}: Two permutation approaches are employed.
(1)~Random label shuffling: Through 1,000 random permutations of ground truth labels, it is tested whether the observed metrics significantly exceed random expectation.
(2)~Circular permutation: The H-score vector is rotated by a random offset $k$ ($H_i \rightarrow H_{(i+k) \bmod N}$), hotspots are re-detected from the rotated scores, and evaluated against the original ground truth.
This approach preserves the spatial autocorrelation structure (cluster pattern) of H-scores while randomizing only the relationship with genomic positions, generating a more conservative null distribution than random label shuffling.
The p-value is calculated as $count / n_{\text{perm}}$ (the proportion of null distribution values that equal or exceed the observed value).

\textit{Pairwise comparison}: A paired bootstrap design that evaluates two methods simultaneously on the same bootstrap resample, testing the statistical significance of hotspot-score differences between methods.
