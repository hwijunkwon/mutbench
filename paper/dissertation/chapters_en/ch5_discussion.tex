% Chapter 5: Discussion
\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results from Chapter~\ref{ch:results} and discusses the methodological implications, limitations, and future research directions of MutBench.
As established in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, MutBench adopts the composite metric design and single-factor isolation principles from general benchmarking methodology~\cite{weber2019benchmarking,boulesteix2017towards} and applies them to viral genomics.

%% ============================================================
\section{Methodological Validity of MutBench}

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Interpretation of Bootstrap Confidence Intervals}
\label{sec:bootstrap_interpretation}

The wide 95\% confidence intervals observed in region-level BCa bootstrap (e.g., MutClust-Hybrid [0.323, 0.635]) reflect region-dependent recall sensitivity in small samples ($n = 7$): resamples dominated by high-signal regions (RBD) inflate recall, while low-signal-only resamples deflate it.
Nevertheless, pairwise bootstrap comparisons show that differences between methods are significant ($p < 0.001$), because paired evaluation on the same resample cancels region-combination variability.
Future work should expand the effective sample size by including additional protein domains (e.g., ORF1ab, N protein).
Having established statistical robustness, the next critical question is whether synthetic benchmark conclusions transfer to real data.

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Performance Gap Between Synthetic and Real Data}
\label{sec:synthetic_real_gap}

The real GISAID 2020--2021 H-score benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:real_gisaid_benchmark}) demonstrated that unconditional extrapolation of synthetic conclusions is not possible.
As shown in Section~\ref{sec:real_gisaid_benchmark}, the sparse distribution of real H-scores (342/29,903 nonzero) fundamentally changes detection dynamics.
Density-based clustering has limited cluster formation under such extremely sparse distributions (HDBSCAN-Auto fails to form clusters), and MutClust-Hybrid's advantage in the synthetic benchmark (hotspot-score 0.778 vs. 0.646) disappears under sparse distributions, with the two methods' performance converging (0.383 vs. 0.386).

Crucially, however, even on real GISAID data the enrichment ratios remain 6.9--8.0-fold above the random baseline, demonstrating that the biological signal captured by top-performing methods is preserved despite the substantial MCC/F1 drop.
The synthetic benchmark retains value precisely because it was designed as a controlled signal-recovery test: its purpose is not to predict absolute real-world performance, but to reveal relative method strengths under conditions where confounders (sparsity, founder effects, sampling bias) are held constant.
Furthermore, the dual-benchmark design---synthetic and real evaluated together---is itself a methodological contribution: it quantifies the gap between theoretical detection capacity and practical performance, information that is unavailable from either benchmark alone.
Therefore, MutBench recommends evaluating both what methods ``can do'' (synthetic) and what they ``actually do'' (real), treating the measured gap as a calibration factor rather than a limitation.

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Independence of Ground Truth and Detection Methods}
\label{sec:circularity}

The synthetic benchmark's ground truth is defined independently of the detection methods (DMS experimental data and convergent evolution literature), ensuring no circularity between evaluation and detection.
Min-max normalization was used as the default throughout MutBench; z-score and rank transformations were not evaluated as they can introduce artifacts in sparse data distributions.
The biological validity of detected hotspots was further supported by 3D structural analysis (Section~\ref{sec:structure_analysis}), which showed 5.92-fold spatial contact enrichment, and by the consistent superiority of domain-specific methods (MutClust variants) over general-purpose alternatives (OPTICS, KDE) in Section~\ref{sec:external_comparison}.
With benchmark validity established, the temporal limitations of the underlying H-score metric warrant examination.

%% ============================================================
\section{Limitations of H-score-Based Detection}

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Limitations of Fixed-Reference Scoring}
\label{sec:hscore_saturation}

The H-score saturation phenomenon observed in temporal validation (Section~\ref{sec:temporal}) reveals a fundamental limitation of reference-based metrics: in 2024--2025 data, all 1,273 Spike positions have non-zero H-scores with a standard deviation of only 0.098, resulting in loss of discriminatory power.
TC-freq achieved the highest enrichment ratio (3.43-fold) on convergent evolution ground truth, suggesting that temporal contrast scoring is a viable direction; the appropriate temporal resolution remains a future challenge.
The saturation phenomenon motivates evaluation against multiple independent ground truth definitions.

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Complementary Evaluation Perspectives}
\label{sec:complementary_gt}

In the multiple ground truth evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:multi_gt}), the Jaccard similarity between DMS-escape and convergent evolution positions was only 0.006, confirming that the two measures capture fundamentally different aspects of variation.
Using only a single ground truth risks overestimating methods that align with a specific biological dimension, and MutBench's multi-ground-truth approach mitigates this.
The result that the best method varies depending on the ground truth suggests that the ``best hotspot detection method'' is context-dependent: for immune evasion studies, methods best suited for the convergent evolution criterion are preferred, while for molecular function studies, methods best suited for the DMS criterion are preferred.
MutBench systematically guides such context-dependent method selection.
The ground-truth-dependent method rankings observed within SARS-CoV-2 foreshadow the pathogen-dependent rankings revealed in the 9-pathogen benchmark.

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Homoplasy-Based Scoring as an Alternative}
\label{sec:homoplasy_scoring}

The founder bias problem identified in Section~\ref{subsec:phylo_correction_poc} --- where H-score conflates founder effects with positive selection ($\rho = -0.876$) --- motivates exploration of phylogeny-based scoring as an alternative to frequency-based metrics.
Fitch parsimony-based homoplasy counting directly addresses this limitation by quantifying the number of independent mutational origins at each position on the phylogenetic tree, rather than relying on aggregate mutation frequency.
A position with high homoplasy (many independent origins) is more likely to be under positive selection, because convergent evolution at the same site across independent lineages implies a shared selective advantage.
Conversely, a founder mutation that arose once and spread through subsequent clonal expansion receives a low homoplasy count regardless of its population frequency, thereby decoupling frequency from selective pressure.

Using 2,000 temporally stratified SARS-CoV-2 sequences from 2020--2021, homoplasy scores were computed via Fitch parsimony on a FastTree-inferred phylogeny.
The D614G founder mutation --- the canonical example of a high-frequency variant driven by a single founder event rather than recurrent positive selection --- received a homoplasy count of only 2 (normalized score 0.038), consistent with its known single-origin, rapid-fixation history.
In contrast, position 476 near the RBD antibody binding interface received the highest count of 53, consistent with known immune evasion pressure at this site.
However, key convergent evolution positions associated with later variants of concern showed weak signals (E484K: 1, N501Y: 0), attributable to the limited temporal coverage of the dataset (pre-Omicron sequences only).
These positions would be expected to accumulate substantially higher homoplasy counts in datasets extending through 2022--2023, when convergent evolution at these sites became prominent across multiple independent lineages.

To evaluate whether homoplasy-based scoring generalizes beyond SARS-CoV-2, the scores were compared against Layer~A ground truth for three pathogens with sufficient phylogenetic diversity.
\phantomsection\label{tab:homoplasy_validation}
For each pathogen, homoplasy scores were computed on publicly available alignments using Fitch parsimony, and discrimination between Layer~A (positive selection) and non-Layer~A positions was assessed via AUC, Mann-Whitney $U$ test, and Cohen's $d$ effect size.
HIV-1 showed significant discrimination (AUC = 0.706, $p = 0.0002$), while HCV failed (AUC = 0.390, parsimony saturation) and H3N2 showed a weak, non-significant trend (AUC = 0.561, $p = 0.147$).

The cross-pathogen results reveal a pattern of pathogen-dependent performance.
HIV-1's strong discrimination confirms that homoplasy scoring captures adaptive evolution signals in pathogens with high sequence diversity and well-separated phylogenetic lineages.
HCV, however, fails entirely because its hypervariable regions (HVR1/HVR2) exhibit such extreme diversity that parsimony reconstruction becomes saturated --- every position mutates so frequently that homoplasy counts lose discriminative power, and the ground truth positions (which are themselves hypervariable) do not stand out from the background.
H3N2 shows partial but insufficient signal for reliable discrimination.

This pathogen-dependent pattern in scoring method performance mirrors the detection-level finding of the main benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:cross_pathogen_structural}): no single scoring approach works universally across all pathogens.
Just as the best detection algorithm varies across pathogens (the interaction effect, $\omega^2 = 0.285$, Section~\ref{subsec:9pathogen_anova}), the optimal scoring strategy also depends on pathogen-specific characteristics such as sequence diversity, evolutionary rate, and phylogenetic depth.
HIV-1's high phylogenetic diversity and deep coalescent times provide the ideal conditions for parsimony-based homoplasy detection, whereas HCV's extreme hypervariability overwhelms the same approach.
This finding provides additional motivation for the PAHD framework's adaptive approach: the selection problem extends beyond detection methods to encompass scoring methods as well.
A fully realized PAHD implementation should therefore jointly optimize both the scoring function and the detection algorithm as a function of pathogen profile, expanding the algorithm space $\mathcal{A}$ from 351 detection-only combinations to include scoring alternatives such as homoplasy, temporal contrast, and protein language model scores.

\section{Cross-Pathogen Generalization and PAHD}

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Implications of Cross-Pathogen Generalization}
\label{sec:cross_pathogen_implications}

As detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, the 9-pathogen benchmark demonstrated that no universally best-performing method exists, with all pathogens exhibiting unique best-performing combinations and substantial interaction effects. This finding has important implications in the context of real-time pathogen surveillance systems such as Nextstrain~\cite{nextstrain2018hadfield}: when a new pathogen or variant emerges, applying a previously optimized combination is inadequate, and adaptive method selection based on pathogen characteristics is required.
The underlying cause is that pathogen-specific genomic characteristics---entropy distribution, proportion of conserved regions, and density of positively selected sites---create fundamentally different signal landscapes for detection methods.
For example, pathogens with high overall mutation rates (e.g., H3N2, where all positions have nonzero H-scores) neutralize the advantage of threshold-based seed detection, while pathogens with compact genomes and few convergent evolution sites (e.g., MERS) favor methods that can detect weak signals in sparse data.
The proof-of-concept simulation further revealed that frequency-based scoring conflates founder effects with positive selection ($\rho = -0.876$), adding another pathogen-dependent dimension: the degree of founder-effect contamination varies with each pathogen's transmission history.
Based on these findings, PAHD (Pathogen-Adaptive Hotspot Detection) is proposed as a conceptual framework for pathogen-specific method selection, formalizable within Rice's algorithm selection problem~\cite{rice1976algorithm}, a framework that has since been extended to automated algorithm selection~\cite{kerschke2019algorithm} and AutoML~\cite{kotthoff2016algorithm}.
Concretely, MutBench's results instantiate Rice's framework as follows: the \textit{problem space} $\mathcal{P}$ consists of pathogens characterized by genomic profiles (alignment length, diversity ratio, ground truth density, etc.); the \textit{algorithm space} $\mathcal{A}$ comprises 351 scoring--detection combinations (9 scoring formulas $\times$ 39 detection algorithms); and the \textit{performance space} $\mathcal{Y}$ is MCC evaluated against the 3-layer ground truth. The interaction dominance ($\omega^2 = 0.285$) statistically demonstrates that the mapping $f: \mathcal{P} \to \mathcal{A}$ is non-trivial---a finding consistent with the intuition of the No Free Lunch theorem~\cite{wolpert1997nfl}, which states that no single algorithm can outperform all others across all possible problem distributions. The PAHD prototype's 5/9 positive MCC result demonstrates that even a simple nearest-neighbor approach in the profile space $\mathcal{P}$ partially recovers this mapping, motivating more sophisticated meta-learning approaches.

The PAHD prototype results (Section~\ref{subsec:pahd_prototype}) demonstrate that coarser prediction granularity recovers up to 57.7\% of oracle performance, though the $n = 9$ limitation persists.
The ESM-2 cross-paradigm validation (Section~\ref{subsec:esm_validation}) further reinforces this finding: ESM-LLR ranked 1st for SARS-CoV-2 and MERS but last for Norovirus and HIV-1, confirming that no single scoring paradigm---whether MSA-based or protein language model-based---dominates across all pathogens.
The concrete implementation of PAHD is proposed as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.
The demonstrated necessity of pathogen-adaptive selection raises the question of how to interpret the absolute performance levels achieved.

\subsection{Upper Bound of Detection Performance}
\label{sec:oracle_mcc_ceiling}

The mean oracle MCC across 9 pathogens is 0.298 (range: 0.088--0.549), which may appear low but is explained by three structural factors.
First, \textbf{position-level exact matching} imposes a strict criterion where a detection off by even one position counts as a false positive.
Second, \textbf{extreme class imbalance} (positive rates mostly below 10\%) constrains the achievable MCC---under a 5\% positive rate, 90\% recall and 30\% precision yield a theoretical MCC of only 0.49.
Third, \textbf{ground truth stringency} penalizes methods that detect genuine but unannotated hotspots, as only literature-confirmed convergent evolution sites are treated as positives.

Despite these constraints, the oracle MCC of 0.298 is unambiguously non-random: the random baseline (10,000 permutations) has a mean of 0.001 (SD = 0.003), placing the oracle approximately 99 standard deviations above chance.
For context, similar position-level evaluation metrics in other bioinformatics benchmarks yield comparable ranges: transcription factor binding site prediction achieves F1 of 0.3--0.5 at single-nucleotide resolution~\cite{weirauch2013evaluation}, and early CASP protein structure prediction achieved GDT-TS scores below 0.3 for difficult targets~\cite{moult2014casp} (though GDT-TS measures structural similarity rather than binary classification, the comparison illustrates that low absolute scores are common in position-level biological prediction).
The oracle MCC of 0.298 is thus consistent with the inherent difficulty of position-level biological prediction tasks.

To quantify the impact of position-exact evaluation, a region-overlap analysis was conducted using $\pm5$ and $\pm10$ position windows (Table~\ref{tab:region_overlap_mcc}).
Under region-overlap evaluation, a detected position counts as a true positive if any ground truth position lies within the specified window, and a ground truth position counts as recalled if any detection lies within the window.
The $\pm5$ window corresponds approximately to a single protein secondary structure element or antigenic epitope ({$\sim$}15 residues), providing a biologically meaningful proximity criterion. This analysis serves as a sensitivity check on the strictness of position-exact evaluation, not as a replacement: position-exact MCC remains the primary metric because it is parameter-free and ensures full reproducibility.
Under $\pm10$ window evaluation, the mean oracle MCC increased from 0.289 to 0.712, a 146\% improvement.
Notably, H3N2 achieved MCC = 0.964 under $\pm10$ (vs. 0.521 exact), confirming that detected hotspots are spatially proximate to known antigenic drift sites even when not exactly overlapping.
This quantifies the conservativeness of position-exact evaluation: the large improvement under relaxed windows indicates that many detections fall within biologically relevant proximity of ground truth positions, rather than being scattered randomly across the genome.

\section{Practical Implications and Fundamental Limitations}

Returning to the three problems identified in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}: the absence of a standardized evaluation framework is addressed by MutBench's three-layer ground truth and hotspot-score metric (Contribution~1); the lack of cross-pathogen generalization evidence is resolved by the four independent lines of statistical evidence (Contribution~2); and the unknown pathogen-dependence of detection methods is quantified by the interaction effect ($\omega^2 = 0.285$, Section~\ref{subsec:9pathogen_anova}) and LOPO failure (0/9), motivating the PAHD framework.

\subsection{Vaccine Escape Position Validation}
\label{sec:vaccine_escape}

To evaluate the practical relevance of MutBench-detected hotspots for public health, the overlap between detected positions and experimentally confirmed vaccine/antibody escape positions was analyzed.
Because hotspot detection methods are ultimately intended to identify positions of functional importance, demonstrating that detected hotspots coincide with known immune escape sites provides direct evidence of biological utility beyond statistical performance metrics.

For SARS-CoV-2, 34 escape positions were curated from published antibody escape mapping studies (Classes 1--4 monoclonal antibodies and NTD supersite).
The best-performing MutBench method (E\_only + KDE) detected 25 of 34 escape positions (recall = 0.735), with a 4.90-fold enrichment over random expectation (Fisher's exact test, $p < 0.0001$).
Across the full method space (351 scoring--detection combinations), all 34/34 escape positions were detected by at least one combination, confirming that the diversity of methods in the benchmark provides comprehensive coverage of functionally important sites.

H3N2 showed borderline enrichment (6/25 antigenic drift positions, enrichment = 2.10$\times$, $p = 0.054$) with entropy-based scoring outperforming H-score, consistent with the founder bias limitation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:hscore_saturation}.
HIV-1 showed poor results because the curated drug resistance positions are located in RT/PR genes, while the benchmark evaluates gp120 envelope protein---a data scope limitation rather than a methodological failure.

These results demonstrate that MutBench hotspots are not merely statistical artifacts but correspond to positions of genuine public health importance.
The finding that no single method captures all 34 escape positions, yet the full method ensemble achieves 100\% recall, provides direct evidence for the value of maintaining a diverse method portfolio---precisely what PAHD aims to systematize.

\subsection{Practical Considerations for Public Health Surveillance}
\label{sec:practical_surveillance}

Considering SARS-CoV-2's generation time (approximately 5 days) and sequence submission delay (median approximately 14 days), a minimum 4-week window is required for real-time TC-freq alerts.
HDBSCAN-Auto (high recall) is suitable for early warning, while MutClust-Hybrid (balanced performance) is suitable for vaccine candidate selection; in retrospective analysis, MutClust-Hybrid's higher recall suggests potential for earlier detection of emerging hotspot positions, though quantitative timing estimates require dedicated temporal validation.

A two-stage detection pipeline---first-stage broad screening with HDBSCAN-Auto followed by second-stage high-precision confirmation with MutClust-Hybrid (Section~\ref{sec:variant_comparison})---maintained precision (0.968) while improving stability to 0.740, achieving the highest hotspot-score (0.784).
This two-stage approach is recommended for vaccine design applications where downstream experimental costs demand robust hotspot selection, whereas single-method approaches suffice for routine surveillance (MutClust-Hybrid) or early warning (HDBSCAN-Auto).
However, in real GISAID 2020--2021 data, HDBSCAN-Auto failed to form clusters due to the sparsity of non-zero positions (342), so the benefits of the two-stage pipeline were not realized.
A fundamental methodological limitation that affects all frequency-based approaches is phylogenetic non-independence.

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Phylogenetic Non-Independence and Scalability Limits}
\label{sec:phylo_limits}

All detection methods in this study implicitly assume independence between sequences; however, SARS-CoV-2 sequences are phylogenetically structured, and frequency-based approaches cannot distinguish positive selection from founder effects (e.g., D614G;~\cite{korber2020tracking}).
The dN/dS filter partially addresses this but is itself susceptible to phylogenetic bias.

Proof-of-concept simulations (Section~\ref{subsec:phylo_correction_poc}) demonstrated that Fitch parsimony-based homoplasy scores correctly rank convergent mutations above founder mutations, and extended TreeTime ML validation (Section~\ref{subsec:treetime_ml_validation}) confirmed equivalent accuracy (AUC = 1.000).
Validation on real GISAID sequences (Section~\ref{subsec:treetime_gisaid_validation}) showed that H-score and homoplasy score capture fundamentally different signals ($\rho = -0.107$, $p = 1.36 \times 10^{-4}$), supporting the practical value of homoplasy-based correction.
The homoplasy sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:homoplasy_sensitivity}) revealed a temporal mismatch between the 2020-H1 sequence data and the Layer~A convergent evolution ground truth, confirming that phylogenetic correction requires temporally aligned data.
Full integration of phylogenetic correction with temporally appropriate sequence sampling remains a key future extension (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\subsection{Representativeness and Sampling Bias of GISAID Data}

Systematic biases exist in the GISAID~\cite{shu2017gisaid}-based input data.
\textbf{Geographic bias}: High-income countries contribute over 80\% of total sequences~\cite{chen2022gisaid}, leading to underrepresentation of unique variants from Africa/South Asia; weekly stratification corrects only for temporal imbalance.
\textbf{Temporal bias}: Sequence accumulation differs substantially between the early pandemic and Omicron periods, and within-week representativeness is not guaranteed.
\textbf{Founder effect}: ``Hitchhiker'' mutations accompanying lineage expansion (e.g., JN.1;~\cite{kaku2024virological}) cannot be distinguished from positively selected positions by frequency-based approaches alone.

\subsection{Ground Truth Heterogeneity Across Pathogens}
\label{sec:gt_heterogeneity}

A key limitation of the 3-layer ground truth framework is the heterogeneity in how Layer~A (positive selection sites) is defined across pathogens.
For SARS-CoV-2 and H3N2, Layer~A positions are phylogenetically confirmed convergent evolution sites---positions where identical mutations arose independently in multiple lineages.
In contrast, HCV Layer~A comprises hypervariable regions (HVR1/HVR2) that exhibit diversifying rather than convergent selection, Norovirus Layer~A consists of epochal variant positions associated with antigenic shift events, and the remaining pathogens use literature-reported immune evasion sites with varying levels of phylogenetic confirmation (Table~\ref{tab:gt_positions}).
This heterogeneity could artificially inflate the method $\times$ pathogen interaction in the ANOVA, because differences in ground truth definition criteria---not only differences in pathogen biology---may contribute to the observed interaction effect ($\omega^2 = 0.285$).
However, this heterogeneity reflects the real-world challenge of hotspot detection: ground truth definitions necessarily depend on pathogen-specific biology, and no single criterion (e.g., convergent evolution alone) is applicable across all pathogens.
The finding that detection performance is pathogen-dependent holds regardless of whether this dependence stems from intrinsic biological differences among pathogens, from differences in ground truth construction, or from their interaction---in all cases, the practical implication is the same: a single detection method cannot be applied uniformly across pathogens without performance degradation.
Additionally, HCV has an effective Layer~B size of 0 (constrained regions could not be mapped within the alignment length), meaning HCV evaluation relies solely on Layer~A. This asymmetry should be considered when interpreting HCV-specific results.

\subsection{Scope Justification: 9 RNA Viruses}

The 9 pathogens were selected to systematically cover 6 major RNA virus families (Coronaviridae, Orthomyxoviridae, Retroviridae, Pneumoviridae, Flaviviridae, Caliciviridae), representing the primary taxonomic diversity of RNA viruses with pandemic potential.
This scope limitation to RNA viruses is an intentional design decision: H-score-based scoring requires sufficient mutation density, which RNA viruses provide due to their high mutation rates ($10^{-3}$--$10^{-5}$ substitutions/site/year), whereas DNA viruses ($10^{-6}$--$10^{-8}$) would require fundamentally different scoring approaches.
Despite the small sample size ($n=9$), the Friedman test achieved $p=0.0015$, indicating that the effect size is large enough to be detected even with limited pathogens.
