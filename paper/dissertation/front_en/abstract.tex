% Abstract
Detecting mutation hotspots in viral genomes is essential for variant surveillance and vaccine design, yet no systematic benchmark framework has existed to compare and evaluate detection methodologies.
Previous studies relied on single pathogens and single detection methods, leaving it unknown which scoring--detection combinations are suitable for which pathogens.

This dissertation presents MutBench, the first systematic benchmark framework for viral mutation hotspot detection.
MutBench comprises three core components:
(1) a three-layer ground truth system integrating convergent evolution signatures as positive selection markers (Layer~A), conserved structural regions under purifying selection as negative controls (Layer~B), and Deep Mutational Scanning (DMS) experimental fitness data as functional validation (Layer~C);
(2) a composite evaluation metric defined as $\text{hotspot-score} = (\text{recall} + \text{precision} + \text{stability}) / 3$; and
(3) a two-stage experimental design in which Stage~1 conducts in-depth analysis on SARS-CoV-2 and Stage~2 performs 8{,}580 evaluations across 11 pathogens (20 scoring formulas $\times$ 39 detection algorithms $\times$ 11 pathogens).

Stage~1 validates the framework on SARS-CoV-2 (MutClust-Hybrid hotspot-score 0.778).
In the Stage~2 cross-pathogen benchmark, the interaction between scoring method and pathogen explains 29.6\% of total performance variance ($\omega^2_{\text{scoring} \times \text{pathogen}} = 0.296$), confirming this as the largest source of variance---surpassing the detection algorithm family main effect ($\omega^2 = 0.013$) by over 20 fold. The Friedman test confirms that no single universally best-performing combination exists ($\chi^2 = 7.69$, $p = 0.990$), and Leave-One-Pathogen-Out (LOPO) cross-validation reveals that all 11 pathogens require different best-performing scoring--detection combinations (generalization success rate 0/11).
Furthermore, detected hotspots show 4.90-fold enrichment for experimentally confirmed vaccine escape positions ($p < 0.0001$), demonstrating practical relevance for public health surveillance.

These results provide statistical demonstration that not only which detection algorithm but, more critically, which biological information source is used determines performance, and that optimal strategies differ fundamentally across pathogens.
A systematic analysis of 20 scoring types spanning six information categories (frequency, MSA-derived, phylogenetic, structural, AI-based, and composite) across 11 pathogens reveals 9 distinct best-performing scoring types, with FUBAR phylogenetic selection scores achieving the highest MCC for H3N2 (0.534), while protein language model scores dominate for SARS-CoV-2 and RSV.
Multi-source integration further reduces the experimental search space for hotspot validation, achieving 4.012$\times$ vaccine escape enrichment for H3N2 ($p = 0.0023$).
