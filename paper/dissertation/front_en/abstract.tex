% Abstract
Detecting mutation hotspots in viral genomes is essential for variant surveillance and vaccine design, yet existing approaches have relied almost exclusively on mutation frequency and Shannon entropy, leaving unexplored a rich landscape of complementary information sources including phylogenetic selection signals, protein language models, and structural features. No systematic comparison has evaluated which of these information types is most effective for which pathogen.

This dissertation presents MutBench, an experimental framework for analyzing which factors influence viral mutation hotspot detection. It systematically compares 10 biological information types (organized into 20 scoring formulas across 6 categories) combined with 14 detection method families spanning 5 categories (threshold-based, signal processing, density/clustering, statistical, and adaptive window), with 39 parameter variants, across 11 RNA viruses, totaling 8{,}580 evaluations.
MutBench comprises three core components:
(1) a three-layer ground truth system integrating convergent evolution signatures as positive selection markers (Layer~A), conserved structural regions under purifying selection as negative controls (Layer~B), and Deep Mutational Scanning (DMS) experimental fitness data as functional validation (Layer~C);
(2) a composite evaluation metric defined as $\text{hotspot-score} = (\text{recall} + \text{precision} + \text{stability}) / 3$; and
(3) a two-stage experimental design in which Stage~1 conducts in-depth analysis on SARS-CoV-2 and Stage~2 performs the large-scale cross-pathogen comparison.

Stage~1 validates the framework on SARS-CoV-2 (MutClust-Hybrid hotspot-score 0.778).
The central finding from Stage~2 is that the optimal biological information source differs fundamentally across pathogens: the interaction between scoring method and pathogen explains 29.6\% of total performance variance ($\omega^2_{\text{scoring} \times \text{pathogen}} = 0.296$), the largest source. The Friedman test confirms that no single universally best-performing combination exists ($\chi^2 = 7.69$, $p = 0.990$), and Leave-One-Pathogen-Out (LOPO) cross-validation reveals that all 11 pathogens require different optimal information sources (generalization success rate 0/11).
Furthermore, detected hotspots show up to 9.36-fold enrichment for experimentally confirmed vaccine escape positions ($p < 0.0001$), and the scoring types optimized by the benchmark also maximize vaccine escape capture across three pathogens (2{,}340 evaluations), demonstrating practical relevance for public health surveillance.

A systematic analysis of the 20 scoring types spanning six information categories (frequency, MSA-derived, phylogenetic, structural, AI-based, and composite) reveals 9 distinct optimal information types across 11 pathogens: FUBAR phylogenetic selection scores achieve the highest MCC for H3N2 (0.534), protein language model scores dominate for SARS-CoV-2 and RSV, and homoplasy scores excel for Norovirus.
Multi-source integration further reduces the experimental search space for hotspot validation, achieving 4.012$\times$ vaccine escape enrichment for H3N2 ($p = 0.0023$), while a compact 4-feature core captures 98\% of full-ensemble performance.
